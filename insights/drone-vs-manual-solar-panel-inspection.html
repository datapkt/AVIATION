Drone vs manual solar panel inspection: which finds more, faster?

How drone thermal inspection compares with walking the rows: coverage, speed, the faults each method finds and misses, the conditions a valid flight needs, and the workflow that uses both.

By the DATA PKT survey and inspection team · Published 23 November 2026 · Updated 24 November 2026 · 11 min read

Short answer: a drone thermal inspection covers every module of a plant in hours, maps each fault to its table and string, and in one study its findings closely matched a manual check of the same plants in a fraction of the time. Manual methods still win at measuring power loss (IV curves), finding cracks (EL imaging) and checking inside boxes. The best practice is to fly first and send the ground team only where the drone points.

What each method actually checks

A manual inspection sends technicians along the rows with a handheld thermal camera, a clamp meter and an IV curve tracer. It measures well, but it measures what the team reaches: on a large plant, that usually means a sample of rows and strings. Handheld thermal imaging is slow over a large plant and some areas get missed, which is why it suits small sites best[10].

A drone inspection flies a radiometric thermal camera and a colour camera over every table. Software stitches the images, finds modules that run warmer than their neighbours and pins each one to its table, string and module. Nothing is sampled: every module is seen in one campaign.

Speed: hours instead of days

The clearest comparison comes from a US study that inspected four plants, 12.5 MW to 74 MW, by drone and compared the results with manual methods on the same sites. Drone inspection took about 10 minutes of flying per MW. When a manual crew was sent to check the drone's findings, its results closely matched the drone data, while the manual work took far longer on every site[5].

Indian labour costs are lower than American ones, so the money saved per MW will be smaller here. The time saved is not: a plant that needs a week of walking for a full thermal check can be flown in a day or two, and the crew is then sent only to the modules that need them.

What thermal imaging can and can't see

A healthy module runs at an even temperature in the sun. When part of it stops converting light to electricity, that part turns the energy into heat, and the thermal camera sees it. Research groups describe the common patterns the same way[7][8]:

  • A single hot cell: one square clearly warmer than the cells around it, often a damaged or shaded cell.
  • A bypass diode pattern: one row or column of cells, usually a third of the module, running warm because a diode has switched that substring out.
  • A whole warm module or string: the module is producing nothing, often an open circuit, a blown fuse or a disconnected string.
  • Soiling and shading: irregular warm areas with soft edges.

Severity varies a lot. In a 2025 field study, short-circuited bypass diodes and heavy soiling produced hotspots of 80 °C to over 100 °C, while modules with cracked cells were hard to tell apart from healthy ones[4]. That is the honest limit of thermography: it finds faults that make heat. Cracks that have not yet cut a cell off need electroluminescence (EL) imaging.

The conditions that make a thermal flight valid

A thermal flight on a cloudy morning produces colourful pictures, not evidence. The standard for outdoor thermography of PV plants, IEC TS 62446-3, sets the conditions[1]:

  • Irradiance of at least 600 W/m² on the modules, so that faults heat up enough to show[2]. The 2025 study found some faults, such as disconnected interconnects, stayed visible only above that level[4].
  • Steady sky: cloud cover of at most 2/8 and wind below about 29 km/h[2].
  • Clean modules, or soiling noted, so dirt is not mistaken for a defect[2].
  • Resolution: a detailed inspection needs at most 3 cm per pixel. With cells of about 16 × 16 cm, that gives around 25 temperature readings per cell; at 5 cm per pixel it drops to about 9[3].
  • Qualified people: the standard expects the thermographer to hold a recognised certification, such as ISO 9712[3].

The standard separates a quick simplified inspection, which looks for patterns, from a detailed inspection, which measures temperatures at the finer resolution[1]. Ask which one you are buying.

Where manual inspection still wins

  • Measuring the loss. A thermal image shows where a fault is. An IV curve trace measures how much power a string is losing, which decides whether a repair or warranty claim is worth it.
  • Cracks and early damage. EL imaging, usually at night, shows cracks and inactive cell areas that thermography cannot[4][7].
  • Electrical parts in boxes. Combiner boxes, inverters and connectors inside enclosures need the door open and a handheld camera.
  • Small rooftops. On a few hundred modules, one technician with a handheld camera can be quicker than planning a flight.

The best practice: drone first, ground team second

The two methods are not rivals. The fastest way to a healthy plant is to let the drone find and map every fault, then send the ground team only where it is needed: to measure, to confirm cracks with EL, and to repair. A second flight after repairs proves they worked and gives you a clean baseline for next year.

How often to inspect

Inspect at commissioning, to catch installation faults while the EPC contractor is still responsible; every year, as routine; after storms or hail; and before warranties expire, so claims are filed in time. A survey of US plant owners found that of the plants doing routine plant-wide inspections, fewer than half did them more than once a year, largely because inspection was slow and costly[9]. A drone removes most of that cost.

Frequently asked questions

Is a drone thermal inspection as accurate as a manual inspection?

For finding faults, yes, and it covers the whole plant. In one study, a manual inspection of the same plants closely matched the drone results but took far longer on every site. Manual tests such as IV curve tracing are still needed to measure how much power a faulty string is losing.

What conditions does a drone thermal inspection of a solar plant need?

IEC TS 62446-3 asks for irradiance of at least 600 W/m² on the modules, little cloud (at most 2/8 of the sky), moderate wind and clean modules. A detailed inspection is flown at a resolution of 3 cm per pixel or finer.

Can thermal imaging find cracked solar cells?

Not reliably. A 2025 field study found modules with cell cracks hard to tell apart from healthy ones in drone thermal images. Electroluminescence (EL) imaging is the better test for cracks, so cracks found by other means should be confirmed with EL.

How long does a drone take to inspect a solar plant?

It depends on plant size, resolution and flight conditions. One US study measured about 10 minutes of flying per MW, and a 74 MW site took 24 hours of drone inspection time including both thermal and colour flights.

How often should a solar plant be inspected by drone?

At commissioning, before the end of the module warranty, after storms or hail, and at least once a year as routine. Many plants inspect less often because manual inspection is slow and costly; a drone makes a yearly plant-wide inspection practical.

What does a drone solar inspection report contain?

A geotagged map of every anomaly, its type and severity, the table, string and module it sits on, thermal and colour images of each, the flight conditions, and a prioritised repair list. Our guide to the solar thermography report lists each item.
