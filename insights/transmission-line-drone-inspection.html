Transmission line drone inspection vs foot patrol: what each one catches

How drone inspection of power lines compares with foot patrols and helicopters: what each can see, how thermal hotspots are graded, the tree clearances India sets by voltage, and how to plan an inspection.

By the DATA PKT survey and inspection team · Published 23 November 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: a foot patrol sees a tower from below and misses what sits on top of the hardware; a helicopter covers hundreds of kilometres a day but with less detail; a drone inspects every clamp, insulator and joint up close, with thermal for hotspots and LiDAR for tree clearance. Use foot patrol for short accessible lines, helicopters for very long corridors, and drones for detailed inspection of most lines in between.

Three ways to inspect a line

Utilities inspect overhead lines in three main ways, and research reviews of the field describe the same split: ground patrol, helicopter, and small drones (with climbing robots as a niche fourth)[8].

  • Foot patrol. A lineman walks or drives the route with binoculars and sometimes a handheld thermal camera. It is the cheapest option per kilometre on short, accessible lines[6], but it sees the tower from below.
  • Helicopter. Fast, covering hundreds of kilometres a day, and good with LiDAR for vegetation clearance and sag; less good on fine detail of each fitting[7]. It stays cost-competitive on very long corridors, roughly 1,000 km or more in a single mobilisation[6].
  • Drone. Slower, covering several kilometres a day in close inspection, but close enough to see each clamp, insulator and fitting, with visual and thermal cameras[7]. Drones sit between the other two: a corridor mode for the line as a whole, and a fine mode that inspects each tower up close[6].

In India, POWERGRID began drone-based patrolling of EHV lines with its own fleet of micro drones; in February 2023 it patrolled about 200 towers that way[1]. Its stated uses are tower inspection, clearance measurement and vegetation management[2]. Other transmission owners, such as IndiGrid, have adopted drone patrolling too[3].

What a drone sees that a patrol misses

The defects that cause outages often sit where a person on the ground can't see them: on top of the cross-arm, behind the insulator string, at the jumper loop. From level with the hardware, a drone camera shows:

  • Insulators: cracked, chipped or flashed discs, and polluted strings[3].
  • Fittings: loose or corroded clamps, missing split pins, damaged spacers and dampers[3].
  • Conductors and earth wire: broken strands, sag, damaged OPGW splice boxes.
  • The tower itself: rust, bent or missing members, and bird nests. Missing members from theft are a known problem; one state utility's inspection tender lists it alongside rugged terrain, cyclones and ageing assets[9].

Thermal: reading a hotspot

A failing joint or loose clamp has higher electrical resistance, so it heats up under load. A thermal camera on a drone shows it as a bright spot long before it fails. The severity is judged by how much hotter it runs than a similar component under similar load, for example the same clamp on the next phase. The widely used ANSI/NETA table sets three bands[4]:

Temperature rise vs similar componentMeaningAction
1–3 °CPossible deficiencyInvestigate
4–15 °CProbable deficiencyRepair as time permits
Over 15 °CMajor discrepancyRepair immediately

Two cautions. First, the readings only mean something when the line carries a real load; thermographers usually ask for at least 40 percent of normal load[5]. Second, the same rise can be ranked differently under other criteria sets, so a good report names the criteria it used[4].

Vegetation and clearance

Trees growing into a line cause tripping and fires. India sets right-of-way widths and minimum clearances between conductors and trees by voltage. The table quoted in a National Green Tribunal order gives, for example, a 35 m right of way and 4.6 m tree clearance at 220 kV, and a 46 m right of way and 5.5 m clearance for a 400 kV single-circuit line[10].

Clearance is not fixed: a conductor sags most at mid-span, on hot days and under heavy load. A drone with LiDAR, or photogrammetry on open ground, measures conductor height and tree height span by span, so trimming crews go only where clearance is short[3][7]. See LiDAR vs photogrammetry for why LiDAR is the better tool for wires.

Planning a drone line inspection in India

  • Scope by tower numbers. Start from the tower schedule, with GPS positions, so every finding is tied to a tower and span.
  • Choose the mode. A corridor flight for vegetation, encroachment and sag; a close inspection of each tower for fittings, insulators and thermal.
  • Load and timing. Thermal flights are planned for periods of high load, and in steady weather.
  • Airspace and safety. Each stretch is checked on the Digital Sky map, and the drone keeps a safe distance from energised conductors under the operator's safety procedure.
  • Deliverable. A tower-wise defect register with photos, severity and action, plus span-wise clearance tables, in a format the maintenance team can load into its own system.

Frequently asked questions

Can drones inspect live transmission lines?

Yes. Drones inspect energised lines without an outage, keeping a safe distance from the conductors. POWERGRID and other utilities in India use drones for patrolling, tower inspection, clearance measurement and vegetation management.

What can a drone find on a transmission line that a foot patrol misses?

Anything on top of or level with the cross-arms: loose or hot clamps, jumpers, cracked or flashed insulator discs, damaged earth wire and OPGW fittings, bird nests and missing tower members. A patroller looking up from the ground often cannot see these.

How is a hotspot on a transmission line classified?

By how much hotter it is than a similar component under similar load. Under the widely used ANSI/NETA criteria, 1–3 °C warrants investigation, 4–15 °C is a probable deficiency to repair as time permits, and over 15 °C is a major discrepancy to repair immediately. The line should carry at least about 40 percent of its rated load.

What is the minimum clearance between a power line and trees in India?

The right-of-way table quoted by the National Green Tribunal gives minimum conductor-to-tree clearances of 3.4 m at 66 kV, 4.0 m at 132 kV, 4.6 m at 220 kV and 5.5 m at 400 kV. Check the current CEA regulations and the utility's own standard for each line.

Is a drone cheaper than a helicopter for line inspection?

For detailed inspection and for corridors of a few hundred kilometres, usually yes. Helicopters stay cost-competitive on very long corridors, roughly 1,000 km or more in one mobilisation, where their speed offsets the hourly cost. Foot patrol remains cheapest for short, accessible lines.

What does a drone transmission line inspection report contain?

A tower-by-tower register: tower number and GPS position, each defect with photos (visual and thermal), its severity and recommended action, vegetation and clearance problems by span, and a summary for the maintenance plan.
