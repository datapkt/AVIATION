Point cloud classification explained: LAS classes, ground filtering and QC

What classifying a LiDAR or photogrammetric point cloud means, the standard ASPRS LAS class codes, how ground filters work and where they fail, how to check the result, and what to put in the scope when you outsource it.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 25 November 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: point cloud classification gives every point a code that says what it hit: ground, vegetation, building, water, wire, noise and so on. The codes come from the ASPRS LAS 1.4 specification. The step that matters most is ground classification, because the terrain model, contours and every earthwork quantity are built from it. Ask for the class list, density, accuracy, tiling and coordinate system in writing, and for a QC report that proves them.

What point cloud classification is

A drone LiDAR flight, or a photogrammetry job, produces millions of 3D points. On their own they are just coordinates. Classification sorts them into the things they represent, by writing a number into the classification field of each point in the LAS file. Software can then show only ground for a bare-earth DTM, or pull out conductors to check clearances.

Classification is usually done in two passes. Automatic routines label most points by geometry (height above a local surface, slope, number of returns) and, for photogrammetric clouds, colour. An operator then checks profiles and 3D views and fixes what the routines got wrong. That second pass decides the quality of everything built afterwards.

The standard ASPRS LAS classes

The LAS format is published by ASPRS; the current edition is LAS 1.4, revision R15 (July 2019)[1]. It fixes what each class number means. The standard codes for the LAS 1.4 point formats used for new work are[1][2].

CodeClassTypical use
0Created, never classifiedRaw points straight from processing; none should remain in a delivered file
1Unassigned (processed, but unclassified)Points checked but not in any class the project needs

Alongside the class number, LAS 1.4 carries four flags that can be set on any point: synthetic (not from the laser, for example digitised from a stereo model), key-point (keep when thinning), withheld (keep in the file but leave out of processing) and overlap (lies in the overlap between flight strips)[2]. That is why codes 8 and 12 are no longer used. Some software still lists 19 as reserved, so check your tools before agreeing a class list.

How ground filtering works

Ground filtering decides which points are the bare earth. Three families of methods are in common use:

  • Progressive TIN densification. The method described by Axelsson and used in TerraScan: the area is divided into a grid, the lowest point in each cell is taken as ground, and those points are triangulated into a first surface. Further points are added if they are close enough to the surface and do not change its angle too much, and the TIN is rebuilt after each pass[11]. In the ISPRS comparison of filters, Axelsson's filter did well on steep slopes[10].
  • Morphological filters. The Simple Morphological Filter (SMRF) of Pingel, Clarke and McBride takes the lowest point in each grid cell, then applies a morphological opening with a window that grows each pass. Cells that drop by more than a height threshold are marked as objects. A slope term relaxes the threshold on steep ground[13].
  • Cloth simulation (CSF). Zhang and colleagues turn the point cloud upside down and let a simulated cloth settle onto it. Where the cloth rests is taken as the ground surface, and points within a set distance of it are classed as ground. The user sets only a few values: cloth resolution, rigidness and an option for steep slopes[12].

No single setting suits a whole project: flat farmland, a hill road and a dense town need different parameters, so large jobs are filtered zone by zone.

Where ground filters go wrong

The ISPRS filter test found the same problem areas across the algorithms it compared[10]. Look there first:

  • Low vegetation. Grass, crops and scrub are hard to separate from the ground, especially on slopes. Left in class 2, they lift the DTM.
  • Steep slopes and sharp breaks. Embankment shoulders, cutting crests, river banks and retaining walls look like objects to a filter, so the edges get shaved off[10]. The cloth filter can also give larger errors on steep slopes, which its optional slope post-processing is meant to reduce[12]. On a slope stability study, a shaved crest changes the geometry the analysis depends on.
  • Bridges and ramps. Filters remove bridges completely, partly or not at all, and in the ISPRS test every filter removed ramps[10]. The cloth filter cannot tell objects joined to the ground, such as bridges, from the ground itself[12]. Decks belong in class 17, with the ground surface running beneath them.
  • Water. Water surfaces return few, scattered points, and some of them are noise. Water is usually classified using breaklines drawn along the banks, and ground points too close to them can go to class 20[5][6].
  • Low noise. One point far below the surface can pull the surface down and cause nearby real ground to be rejected[10]. Noise filtering into classes 7 and 18 comes before ground filtering.
  • Complex built-up areas. Courtyards, stepped buildings and multi-level structures remain difficult for every filter[10].

Photogrammetric vs LiDAR point clouds

The LAS classes are the same for both, but the clouds differ. A LiDAR pulse can return several echoes, so points under trees often reach the ground, and return number and intensity help classification. A photogrammetric cloud has one surface only: what the camera saw. Under canopy there is no ground to classify, and no filter can recover it. Its true colour does help separate grass from bare soil or roads from verges.

Image matching also adds noise on water and plain surfaces and blurs sharp edges. A photogrammetric DTM is reliable on open ground and doubtful under vegetation. Our guide to LiDAR vs photogrammetry gives the field-test figures.

Quality control: how to check a classified cloud

A classified cloud should come with evidence that it is right. Four checks cover most of it:

  1. Visual checks. View the cloud coloured by class, and a hillshade of the ground-only DTM. Pits, spikes and buildings left as bumps show up at once.
  2. Profiles. Cut cross-sections at intervals and wherever the terrain changes: embankments, drains, bridge approaches, cuttings. They show vegetation left in ground, or ground lost at a slope edge.
  3. Checkpoints. Compare the DTM with independent surveyed points that were not used in processing. Under ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards, Edition 2, an accuracy test needs at least 30 checkpoints, up to 120 on large projects. Accuracy is reported as RMSE; the non-vegetated vertical accuracy must meet the specification, and the vegetated accuracy is reported[7].
  4. Class accuracy. The USGS Lidar Base Specification requires every point in its minimum scheme to be properly classified, no points left in class 0 unless they are withheld, and classification that is consistent across the project. Noticeable variation between tiles, swaths or lifts can be cause to reject the whole delivery[5].

First, the flight strips must fit. The same specification checks overlap between swaths: differences up to 8 cm in localised areas are acceptable, while differences over 16 cm that follow the flight lines are not[5].

Deliverables

  • Classified point cloud in LAS 1.4. The USGS specification asks for LAS 1.4-R15 using point data record formats 6 to 10[5].
  • LAZ. A lossless compressed version of LAS that keeps the LAS structure, now an OGC community standard[9]. USGS has moved its own point cloud deliveries to LAZ 1.4[8].
  • DTM and DSM as GeoTIFF rasters: the DTM from ground, the DSM from the highest surface.
  • Contours in DWG, DXF or SHP at the interval the drawings need.
  • A QC report: class list, checkpoint RMSE, swath overlap checks and known problem areas.

What to specify when you outsource classification

A scope that only says "classified point cloud" invites disputes. Put these six items in writing: the classes needed, density, accuracy, tiles, CRS and datum, and formats and QC.

ItemWhat to state
Classes neededThe exact codes, for example 1, 2, 3–5, 6, 7, 9, 17 and 18, plus any project classes from 64 upwards (poles, kerbs, OHE masts). Say which classes are checked for accuracy. USGS lists nine classes as its minimum[6].
DensityMinimum pulse or point density. For reference, USGS QL1 needs at least 8 pulses/m² and QL2 at least 2[6]; drone LiDAR is usually much denser.
AccuracyThe vertical RMSE target on open ground, how vegetated areas are reported, and who surveys the checkpoints[7].
TilesOne non-overlapping tile scheme agreed before work starts, in the same CRS as the data, with tiles that edge-match without gaps[5]. Give tile size and naming.
CRS and datumHorizontal coordinate system and zone, vertical datum or geoid model, and units. In India this is often WGS 84 / UTM with heights tied to the project benchmarks.
Formats and QCLAS or LAZ version and point format, raster cell size, contour interval, CAD layer names, and the QC report contents listed above.

Frequently asked questions

What are the ASPRS LAS classes?

They are the standard class codes in the ASPRS LAS 1.4 specification: 0 created, never classified; 1 unassigned; 2 ground; 3, 4 and 5 low, medium and high vegetation; 6 building; 7 low noise; 9 water; 10 rail; 11 road surface; 13 to 16 wires, towers and insulators; 17 bridge deck; 18 high noise; 19 overhead structure; 20 ignored ground; 21 snow; and 22 temporal exclusion. Codes 64 to 255 are free for project-defined classes.

What is ground classification in LiDAR?

It is the step that decides which points are the bare earth and puts them in class 2. Automatic filters such as progressive TIN densification, morphological filters and cloth simulation do most of it, and an operator then corrects the errors in profiles. The DTM, contours and earthwork quantities are all built from the ground class.

Can a photogrammetric point cloud be classified like LiDAR?

Yes, with the same LAS classes, and its true colour helps. But a photogrammetric cloud only has the surface the camera saw, so under trees and dense scrub there are no ground points to classify. Its terrain model is reliable on open ground and doubtful under vegetation.

How do you check the quality of a classified point cloud?

View it coloured by class and as a hillshade of the ground surface, cut profiles across embankments, drains, bridges and cuttings, and test the terrain model against independent checkpoints. ASPRS Edition 2 calls for at least 30 checkpoints and reports accuracy as RMSE. Classification should be consistent across all tiles.

What should I specify when outsourcing point cloud classification?

The exact class codes you need and which are checked for accuracy, the minimum point density, the vertical accuracy target and checkpoints, a non-overlapping tile scheme, the coordinate system and vertical datum, the output formats such as LAS 1.4 or LAZ, DTM, DSM and contours, and the contents of the QC report.
