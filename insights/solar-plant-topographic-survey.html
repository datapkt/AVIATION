Solar site survey

Drone topographic survey for solar power plants: what developers need before design

The layout, the earthwork quantities, the drainage design and the access roads of a solar plant are all drawn on a terrain model. Here is what that survey should capture, how accurate it needs to be, and what to ask for before the design team starts.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 25 November 2026

Short answer: before a solar plant is designed, the site needs a bare-earth DTM with contours, an orthomosaic, the surveyed boundary, every obstruction that could cast a shadow, and the existing drainage lines inside and around the site. A drone survey on DGPS ground control can supply all of these over a large site in one campaign, provided the accuracy is proved with checkpoints and the data is delivered in the designers' coordinate system and height datum.

Why a solar plant needs a topographic survey

A solar site can look flat on a satellite basemap and still have enough fall, ridges and gullies to change the design. Public elevation data and web maps give a first impression of a site, but they are too coarse to grade a plant or size a drain[7]. The World Bank Group's developer guide lists a detailed site plan, "including site surveys, topographic contours", among the outputs of the feasibility stage[1]. The survey feeds five design decisions.

  1. Table and tracker layout. Fixed-tilt tables and single-axis trackers each have a limit on the slope and the ground undulation they can follow. The DTM shows which blocks fit as they are, which need longer posts or terrain-following structures, and which need grading. The same guide notes that topography together with the geotechnical survey shapes the choice of foundation[1].
  2. Grading and earthwork. Cut and fill volumes come straight from the surface model. Uneven ground can need grading that adds cost and time[7], and a coarse survey can leave a surprise in the earthwork bill. The 2,000 MW Pavagada solar park DPR in Karnataka, for example, proposed levelling only where undulation was high and otherwise following "the contour of the land"[2], a decision that can only be made from a contour plan.
  3. Drainage design. Runoff paths, catchment areas and the positions of culverts and drains are read from the DTM. The Pavagada DPR states plainly that "a detailed terrain map is required towards designing of the drainage system"[2].
  4. Roads and cable routes. Internal roads, inverter and transformer pads, cable trenches and the route to the pooling substation should run on stable ground, clear of natural drainage lines. An engineer writing in PV Tech describes a site where the access route was set "without regard to existing site drainage and soil conditions", and the problems that followed[3].
  5. Shading. Ridges, trees, buildings, transmission towers and poles cast shadows that cost energy every day of the plant's life. The developer guide calls avoiding shading critical, because even small areas of shade can significantly impair output[1], and separates far shading from the horizon from near shading by poles, control rooms and switchyard equipment[1]. The survey should record the position and height of each object, not just its outline.

What the survey should capture

Agree the list before the flight. Adding items later usually means a second site visit.

On the last item: a drone sees the surface, not the subsoil. The developer guide recommends a geotechnical survey before final site selection[1], and a mixture of soil and rock below ground often does not show up on any map[7]. The topographic survey makes the ground investigation better targeted; it is not a substitute for one.

Accuracy and ground control

Vertical accuracy matters more than horizontal on a solar site, because grading, drainage falls and post lengths all depend on levels. Three things decide it.

  • Ground control. Ground control points measured with DGPS tie the model to the project coordinates. On a large block, spread them around the edge and through the middle; on a long access road or transmission route, place them along the full length. RTK or PPK positioning on the drone reduces the number of control points needed but does not remove the need for checkpoints.
  • Checkpoints. Independent points left out of processing, compared with the finished model and reported as RMSE in X, Y and Z. This is the only proof of the accuracy that was achieved. Positional accuracy can be stated against a published standard such as the ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards[8].
  • Datum. The survey, the design and the construction stake-out must use the same coordinate system and the same height datum. A mismatch here shifts every level on the drawings.

Our guide to drone survey accuracy, GSD, GCPs and RTK covers these in detail, and the drone survey glossary explains terms such as DTM, GCP and RMSE.

Drone photogrammetry or LiDAR for a solar site?

Many solar sites in India are open land: barren ground, fallow farmland or sparse scrub. On ground like this, drone photogrammetry on DGPS control gives a DTM, contours and a true-colour orthomosaic in one flight, and is usually the economical choice.

Two conditions change that.

  • Vegetation. Photogrammetry maps the top of whatever it sees. Where dense scrub or tree cover hides the ground, a photo-derived surface sits on the canopy and the DTM is wrong by the height of the bushes. LiDAR can record ground returns through gaps in the vegetation and is the better tool there; the other options are clearing before the survey or ground survey of the covered patches.
  • Very uniform terrain. Bare sand, salt flats and freshly graded earth give photogrammetry few features to match. Lower flights, more overlap and more ground control help. LiDAR does not rely on image texture, so it is less affected.

On a mixed site, one option is photogrammetry for the open blocks and LiDAR for the covered strips and drainage lines. Our comparison of LiDAR vs photogrammetry sets out the trade-offs with published accuracy figures.

Hydrology and drainage basics for solar plants

Improperly planned drainage and erosion control at large solar plants is an area under close scrutiny. It leads to drainage and erosion problems after construction and to reduced profits over the life of a facility[3]. The same author advises a detailed hydrology assessment for large sites of 50 MW and above, and warns not to assume that large, sudden storms can pass over a plant without damage from water depth and velocity[3]. Flooding can damage electrical equipment mounted near ground level and increase the risk of erosion around foundations[1].

The DTM is the starting point for that assessment. In GIS, the steps are standard:

  1. Fill sinks. Small false depressions in the model trap simulated flow and are filled first to give a conditioned DTM[5].
  2. Flow direction. Water leaves each cell in the steepest downslope direction[5], so the model gives the direction of flow everywhere on the site.
  3. Flow accumulation and flow paths. Counting how many upstream cells drain into each cell shows where flow concentrates; cells with enough accumulated flow form the drainage network[5].
  4. Catchments. From chosen outlet points, such as a proposed culvert or the site boundary, the catchment draining to each point is delineated[5].

Catchment area, flow length and fall then go into the design discharge. For small catchments, Indian road practice uses a form of the rational formula, in which peak runoff depends on the catchment area, a runoff coefficient and the critical rainfall intensity, set by the time of concentration[4]. IRC:SP:13 asks for catchment area, length and fall from survey maps, and recommends a traverse along the watershed for catchments smaller than 1.25 sq km[4]. It also cautions that empirical formulae should be used with due caution and in consultation with an expert[4]. Other manuals limit the rational method to small basins; the Iowa DOT design manual, for one, restricts it to drainage basins of 160 acres or less[6]. The hydrologist chooses the method; the survey has to supply the inputs.

Two points follow for the survey scope.

  • Survey beyond the boundary. Water that reaches the plant often comes from land upstream of the fence. If the survey stops at the boundary, the external catchment has to be estimated from coarser data. Agree a buffer, or a separate lower-resolution flight of the upstream area.
  • Keep the natural drainage where possible. The Pavagada DPR noted that "essentially the natural slope and drain is maintained for drainage in the solar field", with drains run parallel to the roads[2]. Mapping the existing nallahs and their sections accurately is what makes that approach possible.

Deliverables checklist

Ask for these in the scope, with formats the design team can open directly:

  • Orthomosaic as GeoTIFF, with the GSD stated.
  • DTM and DSM as GeoTIFF rasters, with cell size stated.
  • Point cloud as LAS or LAZ, classified at least into ground and non-ground.
  • Contours, spot levels, boundary, obstructions and existing drainage as DWG for CAD and SHP for GIS, on agreed layers.
  • Drainage layers: flow paths, catchment boundaries and outlet points as SHP, if the hydrology work is in scope.
  • Ground control and checkpoint list with coordinates, descriptions and photographs of each point.
  • Accuracy report with RMSE in X, Y and Z from independent checkpoints.
  • Survey report stating the coordinate system, height datum, equipment, flight dates and any areas of reduced confidence, such as vegetated patches.

Our guide to drone survey deliverables explains what each output is for, and our GIS mapping and data processing page covers layer preparation and GIS to CAD work.

Common mistakes

  • Designing on satellite or public elevation data. Fine for screening sites, too coarse for grading or drainage.
  • Treating the DSM as the ground. On a site with scrub, a surface model from photos is a canopy model. Ask how the DTM was filtered and where confidence is low.
  • No checkpoints. Ground control without independent checkpoints gives a model whose accuracy has not been proved.
  • Mismatched height datum. Survey levels on one datum and design levels on another shift every grading and drainage level.
  • Stopping at the fence. Upstream catchments decide how much water arrives at the plant.
  • Missing obstruction heights. A tree or pole mapped only as a point cannot be used in a shading study.
  • Survey too early or too late. If the site is cleared or partly graded after the survey, the model no longer matches the ground. Plan a check flight before design freeze.

What decides the cost

For a solar site, the main cost factors are the area and whether it is one block or several, the accuracy and GSD required, the amount of ground control, vegetation cover (which decides whether LiDAR is needed), the deliverables and the location. Frequently asked questions

Why does a solar plant need a topographic survey?

The layout of tables or trackers, the grading and earthwork quantities, the drainage design, the internal roads and cable routes, and the shading study are all drawn on the terrain model. Public elevation data and satellite maps are too coarse for these, so a site-specific survey with a bare-earth DTM and contours is needed before design.

What should a solar site survey include?

A bare-earth DTM, contours at an agreed interval, spot levels on very flat ground, a georeferenced orthomosaic, the surveyed boundary, obstructions with their heights, and existing drainage lines inside and upstream of the site. It should also record surface signs such as rock outcrops, erosion and waterlogging to guide the geotechnical investigation.

Is a drone survey accurate enough for solar plant design?

Yes, when it is flown on DGPS ground control, at a GSD suited to the accuracy target, and proved with independent checkpoints reported as RMSE in X, Y and Z. The survey must also use the same coordinate system and height datum as the design team.

Should I use LiDAR or photogrammetry for a solar site?

On open, sparsely vegetated land, drone photogrammetry usually gives the DTM, contours and orthomosaic economically. Where dense scrub or trees hide the ground, LiDAR is the better tool because it can record ground returns through gaps in the vegetation. Mixed sites can combine the two.

How is a drainage study for a solar plant done from the survey?

The DTM is conditioned by filling false sinks, then flow direction, flow accumulation and catchment boundaries are computed in GIS. Catchment area, flow length and fall then feed a design discharge method, such as the rational formula in IRC:SP:13 for small catchments, chosen by the hydrologist. The survey should extend beyond the boundary to cover upstream catchments.
