Landslides and slopes

Landslide survey in India: methods, standards and what it costs

How a landslide survey is done in India: drone LiDAR, seismic refraction, geological mapping, stability analysis, IS codes and what decides the cost.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 2 November 2026

Short answer: a landslide survey finds which slopes may fail, why, and what will hold them. In India it normally combines four things: a drone LiDAR terrain model, seismic refraction and boreholes for the ground below, geological mapping of rock and joints, and a stability analysis in dry, monsoon and earthquake conditions. The result is a hazard map, a priority list and the design inputs for drainage, soil nails, anchors, mesh and rockfall barriers.

Why landslide surveys matter in India

The Geological Survey of India estimates that about 12.6% of India's land area, outside snow-covered ground, is prone to landslides, across the Himalaya, the Northeast, the Western Ghats and the Nilgiris. Heavy monsoon rain, steep cut slopes beside new roads and railways, weathered rock and earthquakes combine to trigger failures every year. A survey before protection works are chosen puts money where the risk actually is.

The four parts of a landslide survey

1. Terrain: drone LiDAR and 3D models

Slopes beside hill roads are usually under trees. Drone LiDAR records the ground through gaps in the canopy, giving a bare-earth terrain model, contours, slope-angle maps and cross-sections. It also shows cracks, scarps, old slide scars and drainage paths. Repeat flights compare surfaces over time to measure movement. See LiDAR vs photogrammetry.

2. Subsurface: seismic refraction and boreholes

Seismic refraction tomography measures how fast sound travels through the ground to show the thickness of loose cover, the depth to weathered and fresh rock, and weak or saturated zones, continuously along each profile. Boreholes give samples and laboratory strength at chosen points. See seismic refraction survey.

3. Material: engineering geological mapping

Geologists map rock types and joints, measure joint orientations, test strength in the field and classify the rock mass with RMR, GSI and SMR. Kinematic analysis on stereonets shows whether planar sliding, wedge sliding or toppling is possible.

4. Stability analysis and hazard zonation

Geometry from LiDAR, layering from seismic refraction and strength from geology go into a limit-equilibrium analysis that gives a factor of safety for each critical section: dry, saturated (monsoon) and earthquake (pseudo-static, using the IS 1893 seismic zone). Stretches are then ranked Low, Medium, High or Critical.

Indian standards and guidelines

  • NDMA guidelines on the management of landslides and snow avalanches: the national framework for hazard assessment and mitigation
  • IS 14496 (Part 2): preparation of landslide hazard zonation maps in mountainous terrain
  • IS 1893: seismic zones and design seismic coefficients
  • IS 11315: quantitative description of rock discontinuities
  • IS 13365: rock mass rating (RMR)
  • IS 8764: point-load strength of rock
  • IS 15681: seismic refraction method
  • IRC:SP:55: traffic management in road work zones; Drone Rules, 2021 for drone operations

What you should receive

  • LiDAR point cloud, DTM, contours, orthophoto, slope maps, cross-sections and 3D models
  • Seismic refraction field data, tomograms and interpreted sections
  • Geological maps and sections, joint data, stereonets, RMR/GSI/SMR, strength parameters
  • Stability analysis for each critical section, hazard zonation map and priority list
  • Remedial recommendations and protection design inputs, or the protection design itself

What decides the cost

  1. Length or area: kilometres of corridor or hectares of slope.
  2. Access and vegetation: remote hill roads, forest and traffic slow field work.
  3. Number of seismic profiles and boreholes: often one profile per kilometre plus extra at critical slopes.
  4. Detailed geology: how many locations need mapping and testing.
  5. Accuracy and deliverables: point density, section spacing, 3D models.
  6. Design scope: design inputs only, or full protection design with drawings and quantities.

An example: 66 km of road-side slopes in Assam

DATA PKT investigated about 66 km of road-side slopes in Assam, in seismic Zone V with heavy monsoon rain: drone LiDAR of the full corridor, 66 seismic refraction profiles, geological mapping at 7 critical locations and a stability report with stretch-wise hazard ranking and protection design inputs, in about nine weeks. Frequently asked questions

What is a landslide survey?

A study of a slope or corridor to find where it may fail, how, and what will stabilise it. It usually combines a terrain survey (often drone LiDAR), subsurface investigation (seismic refraction and boreholes), geological mapping and a stability analysis, and ends with a hazard map and remedial design.

Which Indian standards apply to landslide and slope investigation?

Common references include IS 14496 (Part 2) for landslide hazard zonation, IS 1893 for seismic zones and coefficients, IS 11315 for rock discontinuity surveys, IS 13365 for rock mass rating, IS 8764 for point-load testing, IS 15681 for seismic refraction, and IRC:SP:55 for road work-zone safety. The NDMA guidelines on landslide management set the national framework.

How long does a landslide survey take?

It depends on length and access. As an example, investigating about 66 km of road-side slopes in Assam took about nine weeks, including about three weeks in the field.

What decides the cost of a landslide survey?

Corridor length or slope area, access and vegetation, the number of seismic profiles and boreholes, how many locations need detailed geology, the accuracy and deliverables required, and whether protection design is included. Send the KML and your objectives for a scoped quote.

Can a drone survey alone assess landslide risk?

No. A drone LiDAR survey shows the shape of the slope, cracks and scarps, and repeat flights show movement, but it cannot see soil thickness or rock strength. Those need seismic refraction, boreholes and geological mapping before a factor of safety can be calculated.
