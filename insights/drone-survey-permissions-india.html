Drone survey permissions in India: Digital Sky, airspace zones and what a client should check

Before a drone survey can start, the drone, the pilot and the airspace over your site all have to be in order. This guide explains the Drone Rules, the airspace map on Digital Sky, and the questions a client should ask a survey vendor.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 25 November 2026

Short answer: a commercial drone survey in India needs a drone registered with a Unique Identification Number (UIN), a pilot holding a Remote Pilot Certificate (RPC), third-party insurance, and a check of the site on the Digital Sky airspace map. In a green zone no flight permission is needed up to 400 ft. Yellow zones need air traffic control permission and red zones need Central Government permission.[1][2]

Rules change. Check before every flight. Zone boundaries are updated and temporary red zones can be declared at short notice. The Ministry of Civil Aviation says anyone planning to fly should check the latest airspace map for changes before operating.[2] This guide is general information as of November 2026, not legal advice.

1. Which rules apply in 2026

Civil drone operations in India are governed by the Drone Rules, 2021, notified by the Ministry of Civil Aviation on 25 August 2021. They replaced the earlier UAS Rules with a much shorter set of approvals, made most permissions self-generated on the Digital Sky platform, and cover drones up to 500 kg.[1] The rules have been amended since:

  • Drone (Amendment) Rules, 2022 (G.S.R. 108(E), 11 February 2022) replaced the remote pilot licence with a Remote Pilot Certificate issued by an authorised remote pilot training organisation.[4]
  • Drone (Amendment) Rules, 2023 (G.S.R. 715(E), 27 September 2023) let applicants for a Remote Pilot Certificate use any government-issued identity and address proof instead of a passport.[5]
  • A further amendment dated 21 August 2024 is listed by the Ministry in Parliament;[6] it extended the same identity relaxation to drone registration and transfer forms.[15]

Two newer changes matter in practice. First, regulatory services such as drone registration, pilot certification and type certification have moved from Digital Sky to DGCA's eGCA portal, while operational services such as the airspace map and flight permissions remain on Digital Sky.[7] Second, in September 2025 the Ministry published a draft Civil Drone (Promotion and Regulation) Bill, 2025 for consultation, which would replace the Drone Rules with a law.[13] Until a new law is passed and notified, the Drone Rules, 2021 as amended apply. Check the current position before you sign a contract.

2. The drone: registration, UIN and type certificate

No one may operate a drone without first registering it and getting a Unique Identification Number (UIN).[3] The UIN is marked on the drone and links it to its owner, so a client can ask for it and check it against the aircraft on site. Drones are grouped by maximum all-up weight including payload:[3]

CategoryAll-up weightTypical survey use
NanoUp to 250 gNot used for survey work
MicroMore than 250 g, up to 2 kgSmall mapping drones and inspection quadcopters
SmallMore than 2 kg, up to 25 kgMost RTK mapping drones, LiDAR and fixed-wing survey drones
MediumMore than 25 kg, up to 150 kgSpecialist heavy payloads
LargeMore than 150 kgNot used for survey work

A type certificate is needed for a drone model to be operated in India; nano drones are exempt.[1] Type certificates are issued under the Certification Scheme for Unmanned Aircraft Systems, notified on 26 January 2022 and developed by the Quality Council of India, with testing by approved certification bodies.[8] For a client, the practical point is simple: the survey drone should be a certified model with a valid UIN, not an unregistered import.

3. The pilot: Remote Pilot Certificate

The person flying must hold a Remote Pilot Certificate (RPC). Since 2022 it is issued by a DGCA-authorised remote pilot training organisation (RPTO) after training and a test, and it is generated through the online platform.[4] Only nano drones and micro drones flown for non-commercial use are exempt,[3] so a commercial survey always needs a certified pilot. Applicants must be between 18 and 65 years old and have passed class 10.[9] The certificate states the category and class of drone it covers, so check that it matches the drone being flown on your site.

4. The airspace map: green, yellow and red zones

The Ministry of Civil Aviation published India's airspace map for drones on 24 September 2021. It is free to view on Digital Sky without a login, and it divides the country into three zones.[2]

ZoneWhere it appliesWhat is needed
Green zoneUp to 400 ft (about 120 m) above ground where the area is not yellow or red; up to 200 ft (about 60 m) in the band 8 to 12 km from an operational airport perimeterNo flight permission needed. Registration, pilot certificate and the airspace check still apply
Yellow zoneAbove 400 ft in a green zone; above 200 ft in the 8 to 12 km band; all heights in the band 5 to 8 km from an operational airport perimeterPermission from the air traffic control authority for that airspace: AAI, IAF, Navy or HAL
Red zoneNo-drone zones marked on the map. Close to operational airports, inside the 5 km band, the map typically shows redPermission from the Central Government

The yellow zone around airports was cut from 45 km to 12 km from the airport perimeter when the 2021 rules came in.[1][2] In plain terms, around an operational airport:

  • 12 km and beyond: normal green-zone rules, up to 400 ft, unless the map shows otherwise.
  • 8 to 12 km: green up to 200 ft, yellow above 200 ft. Many mapping flights can be planned below 200 ft, but the GSD and overlap must still meet the specification.
  • 5 to 8 km: yellow at every height, so every flight needs ATC permission.
  • Inside 5 km: check the map. This area is typically shown as red.

These distances are measured from the airport perimeter, not the terminal, and military airfields count too. The map, not a rule of thumb, decides. Long corridors such as highways and railway lines often cross several zones, so each stretch has to be checked separately.

5. Getting permission in yellow and red zones

For a yellow zone, permission comes from the air traffic control authority for that airspace: the Airports Authority of India, the Indian Air Force, the Navy or HAL, depending on the airport.[2] For a red zone, the Central Government must give permission.[2] Requests are made online through Digital Sky, with the flight area, height, timing and drone details.

Two things often surprise clients. First, approval is not instant: a request for a yellow or red zone needs lead time, so fix the survey dates only after the zone check. Second, state governments, union territory administrations and police can declare temporary red zones for events, VIP visits or security reasons. These can appear on the map at short notice and override an approval planned earlier.[16] This is why the airspace check is repeated on the day of the flight.

6. Critical infrastructure, borders and site access

Airspace permission is only one layer. Sites near defence installations, international borders, nuclear and strategic facilities, ports and some government complexes are likely to sit in yellow or red zones, and may need further security clearances from the agency concerned. Even in a green zone, the asset owner usually has its own rules:

  • Railways, metros and highway authorities may require a block, a work permit or an escort for flights over live lines and carriageways.
  • Power utilities set safe distances from energised conductors and may need the line owner's consent.
  • Refineries, plants and private campuses control entry and may restrict cameras.

Put these site permissions in the scope and the schedule. A clear airspace map does not mean the flight can go ahead on the day.

7. Insurance

Under the Drone Rules, third-party insurance of drones other than nano drones is governed by the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, under which third-party cover is compulsory.[14] Ask the vendor for the policy, its validity and whether it covers the drone and the location being flown. Some contracts also ask for cover for the client's assets.

8. Geospatial data: who may collect it, and where it is stored

The Department of Science and Technology's guidelines of 15 February 2021 removed the need for prior approval, security clearance or a licence to acquire and produce geospatial data inside India, subject to a negative list of sensitive attributes.[10][11] The key points for a drone survey are:

  • Indian entities may collect and produce geospatial data at any accuracy, with self-certification used to show compliance.[10]
  • The threshold is one metre horizontal and three metres vertical. Data finer than this may be created and owned only by Indian entities, and must be stored and processed on servers in India.[10]
  • Foreign companies, and foreign-owned or controlled Indian companies, may license finer data from Indian entities only to serve customers in India.[10]

Survey-grade drone data is far finer than the threshold, so it should be held and processed in India. The National Geospatial Policy, 2022 builds on the same approach.[12] Data rules are separate from airspace rules: being free to map does not mean being free to fly.

9. What a client should ask a survey vendor

  1. The UIN of each drone that will fly, and its type certificate or certified model.
  2. The Remote Pilot Certificate of each pilot, valid for that drone category.
  3. A zone check for your site from the Digital Sky map, stretch by stretch for corridors, with the permission plan for any yellow or red section.
  4. Who applies, and the lead time before dates are fixed.
  5. Third-party insurance in force for the survey period.
  6. Site permissions: whose consent, blocks or escorts are needed, and who arranges them.
  7. Data handling: is the company an Indian entity, where are raw and processed data stored and processed, who owns them, and how are sensitive features treated?
  8. A plan for no-fly days: what happens to the schedule if a temporary red zone or bad weather stops the flight?

These sit alongside the technical checks in our guide on how to choose a drone survey company. Permission lead times and site access are also part of what decides the cost of a drone survey. Terms such as UIN, RPC and GSD are explained in our drone survey glossary.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need permission for a drone survey in India?

It depends on where the site is. In a green zone on the Digital Sky airspace map, a registered drone flown by a certified pilot needs no flight permission up to 400 ft (about 120 m) above ground. In a yellow zone, permission is needed from the air traffic control authority for that airspace, and in a red zone from the Central Government. The drone must still carry a UIN and the pilot a Remote Pilot Certificate.

What are the green, yellow and red zones for drones?

They are the zones of the airspace map published on Digital Sky in September 2021. Green zones allow flights up to 400 ft without permission, and up to 200 ft in the band 8 to 12 km from an operational airport perimeter. Yellow zones, including all airspace 5 to 8 km from an airport perimeter, need air traffic control permission. Red zones are no-drone zones that need Central Government permission.

Who gives permission to fly a drone in a yellow zone?

The air traffic control authority that controls that airspace, such as the Airports Authority of India, the Indian Air Force, the Navy or HAL, depending on the airport. The request is made online through the Digital Sky platform, so plan for it before fixing survey dates.

Does a survey drone pilot need a licence in India?

The pilot needs a Remote Pilot Certificate. Since the Drone (Amendment) Rules, 2022 it is issued by a DGCA-authorised remote pilot training organisation through the online platform, and it replaced the earlier remote pilot licence. Only nano drones and micro drones flown for non-commercial use are exempt, so a commercial survey needs a certified pilot.

Do Indian companies need permission to collect drone mapping data?

Under the Department of Science and Technology guidelines of 15 February 2021, Indian entities need no prior approval, security clearance or licence to acquire and produce geospatial data inside India, subject to a negative list of sensitive attributes. Data finer than the threshold accuracy must be stored and processed in India. Airspace permission is a separate requirement.
