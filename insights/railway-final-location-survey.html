Railway final location survey (FLS) by drone and LiDAR: what it covers

The final location survey fixes a new railway line on the ground and produces the plans, sections and estimates the project is sanctioned on. Here is what it must deliver under the Indian Railways Engineering Code, and where drone photogrammetry and LiDAR with DGPS control fit.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 25 November 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: a final location survey (FLS) is the last and most detailed survey before a railway project is sanctioned. It fixes the centre line, curves and gradients, and produces detailed plans, longitudinal and cross-sections, bridge and yard plans and the data for the detailed estimate[1][2]. A drone or aerial LiDAR survey, tied to DGPS control and bench marks, supplies most of the topography; centre-line pegging, pillars, soil investigation and river studies remain ground work.

Where the FLS sits in railway project planning

The Indian Railways Code for the Engineering Department links each survey stage to a report. A reconnaissance survey supports a feasibility study, a Preliminary Engineering-cum-Traffic Survey (PETS) leads to a Techno-Economic Survey Report, and a final location survey leads to the Project Report, which carries the detailed estimate and the abstract cost of the project[2].

StagePurposeTypical survey workReport
ReconnaissanceIs the project worth investigating further?Maps, imagery, site visits, waterway at major crossings[1]Feasibility report[2]
PETS / traffic studyCompare routes, test viability, estimate cost closelyRoute traverses; photogrammetry where suitable photographs exist[1]; traffic studyTechno-Economic Survey Report[2]
Final location surveyFix the alignment and prepare plans for construction and sanctionCentre line, levels, cross-sections, bridges, yards, land, soilProject Report with detailed estimate[2]
Detailed estimate and DPRSanction of the project and award of worksQuantities and costs worked out from the FLS drawingsDPR in the Railway Board format[3]

The order matters. In 2025 the Railway Board told zonal railways to get the traffic study done before going ahead with a final location survey, so that money is not spent surveying a line that is not viable[5]. Its revised FLS rates now include the traffic study within the FLS cost[4]. Under the Railway Board's arrangements for empanelled consultants, the consultant first does the traffic study to test viability, then goes on to the FLS and the DPR[3].

FLS and DPR work covers new lines, doubling and multi-tracking, gauge conversion and yard remodelling[3]. Yard remodelling overlaps with the Engineering Scale Plan (ESP), which shows an existing yard to scale before changes are designed.

What the Engineering Code asks an FLS to deliver

Chapter IV of the Engineering Code sets out the final location survey in detail. The survey should be comprehensive enough to give the information needed for the detailed plans and sections and for an accurate estimate of cost, and it should secure the most economical alignment[1]. The main requirements are below.

Alignment, curves and gradients

  • Centre line: measured in chains of 20 metres, with pegs at every chain and larger pegs at every 100 metres[1].
  • Permanent marks: masonry pillars at tangent points and at intervals along the line, so the alignment can be set out again at construction[1].
  • Curves: defined by degree and radius, with transition curves where the curvature changes; gradients are expressed per 100 metres and compensated for curvature[1].
  • Levels: bench marks at intervals of not more than one kilometre and at the sites of important bridges, referred to mean sea level[1].

Plans and sections

The Code lists the set of plans and sections a project should have: a general map, an index map, an index plan and section, detailed plans and sections, plans and cross-sections, station yard plans, drawings of structures and plans of junction arrangements[1]. Some of the scales it gives:

DrawingScale in the CodeWhat it shows
Index plan and section0.5 km to 1 cm horizontal, 10 m to 1 cm verticalThe whole line, with important bridges, rivers and spans
Detailed plans and sections (L-section)50 m to 1 cm horizontal, 5 m to 1 cm verticalAll features within 100 m each side of the centre line, including village boundaries; ground and formation levels
River plans and cross-sectionsPlan at 50 m to 1 cm; sections at 5 m to 1 cmRivers needing a waterway of 110 sq. metres or more
Station yard and junction plans10 m to 1 cmStations needing special design and junction layouts

All scales above are from Chapter IV of the Engineering Code[1]. The longitudinal section carries two sets of levels, the ground and the formation, and cross-sections are referenced on it[1]. Larger scales may be used in difficult country.

Bridges, culverts, soil and land

Every waterway crossing needs a site study: catchment, flood levels, scour and the size of opening. Large bridges need skeleton drawings for the estimate, and the Code asks for bunds and irrigation works on the route to be investigated, along with any diversion they need[1]. In hilly country a geological investigation is needed, and soil samples are taken systematically along the route and at borrow areas[1]. Land plans, drawn over village and revenue maps, show the land to be acquired and the boundaries it crosses; the detailed plan already shows village land boundaries within 100 metres of the centre line[1].

How drone photogrammetry and LiDAR fit an FLS

The Code was written for theodolite traverses and levelling, but it already allowed photogrammetric techniques for the preliminary survey where suitable aerial photographs were available[1]. Current Railway Board instructions for FLS and DPR consultants go further: they say technological tools such as LiDAR survey and satellite imagery on the BISAG portal should be used for survey work, that the alignment should be planned on the BISAG platform, and that a drone project video should be made where required[3].

High-speed rail shows the method at scale. NHSRCL used helicopter-mounted aerial LiDAR, combining laser data, GPS data, flight parameters and photographs, to map the Delhi–Varanasi corridor as a 3D topographic map 50 metres either side of the proposed alignment[6]. On the Mumbai–Ahmedabad corridor the aerial LiDAR ground survey took 12 weeks, against 10 to 12 months by traditional methods, and fed the design of the vertical and horizontal alignment, structures, stations, land requirement and affected plots[7]. Drones and helicopters were also reported in use for the FLS of the Sambalpur–Gopalpur line in Odisha in 2020[8].

For a conventional line, a drone does the same job over a narrower strip, with lighter logistics than a helicopter. The workflow is:

  1. Control first. A DGPS control network along the corridor, tied to the project datum and to bench marks levelled from mean sea level.
  2. Flight. Photogrammetry for open country and built-up areas; drone LiDAR where vegetation, cuttings or hill slopes hide the ground. Many corridors use both.
  3. Processing. Orthomosaic, digital terrain model and contours, plus a classified point cloud from LiDAR.
  4. Engineering extraction. Longitudinal section along the chosen centre line, cross-sections at the required interval, feature mapping within the corridor and land plans over revenue maps.
  5. Ground work that stays on the ground. Pegging and pillars, river and bridge site studies, soil and geological investigation, and checks of features the aerial data cannot see.

The main gain is that the alignment can be moved on the terrain model without resurveying. Several options can be tested, curves and gradients adjusted, and the sections regenerated before anything is pegged. The comparison with ground methods is covered in drone survey vs DGPS and total station.

Accuracy and ground control

An FLS is only as good as its control. On a linear corridor, ground control points are spread along the full length and alternate on both sides of the alignment, with a pair at each end, so the model does not bend or tilt along its length. Checkpoints are measured the same way but held out of processing, so the accuracy can be proved.

  • Datum: plan coordinates in the project system, and heights as orthometric heights tied to bench marks, not raw GNSS ellipsoidal heights.
  • Chainage: every section, plan and point in the data should carry chainage, so the drawings, the pegs on the ground and the estimate agree.
  • Accuracy report: residuals at independent checkpoints reported as RMSE in X, Y and Z.
  • Vertical accuracy matters most. Earthwork quantities come from the difference between ground and formation levels, so a small bias in the terrain model becomes a large error in the estimate.

How GSD, GCP spacing and RTK or PPK positioning set the achievable accuracy is explained in how accurate is a drone survey.

Common pitfalls

  • Too narrow a corridor. If the strip only covers the first alignment, every shift needs a new flight. Fly wide enough for realignments and for the 100 metres each side the Code's detailed plan needs[1], more where yards or junctions will be built.
  • Photogrammetry under trees. A photogrammetric model maps the canopy, not the ground. Use LiDAR or ground survey in forests, orchards and overgrown cuttings, or the L-section will be wrong; see LiDAR vs photogrammetry.
  • Mixed height systems. Ellipsoidal GNSS heights mixed with levelled bench marks give formation levels that do not match the ground at construction.
  • Water and river beds. Neither LiDAR nor photographs see through water. River cross-sections, bed levels and flood levels need ground survey, bathymetry and local records.
  • No independent checkpoints. Without them the accuracy of the terrain model is unproven, and the earthwork estimate inherits the doubt.
  • Hidden services. Buried cables, pipelines and drains do not appear in aerial data. Utility records and ground checks are still needed for the shifting estimate[3].
  • Land plans on the wrong base. Revenue maps must be fitted carefully to the orthomosaic; a poor fit puts the land boundary in the wrong field.

Frequently asked questions

What is a final location survey in railways?

A final location survey (FLS) is the last and most detailed survey before a railway project is sanctioned. It fixes the centre line, curves and gradients on the ground and produces the detailed plans, longitudinal and cross-sections, bridge and yard plans and data for the detailed estimate in the Project Report, as set out in Chapter IV of the Indian Railways Engineering Code.

What is the difference between PETS and FLS?

A Preliminary Engineering-cum-Traffic Survey (PETS) compares routes and tests whether the project is viable, and leads to a Techno-Economic Survey Report. The final location survey follows on the chosen route, fixes the alignment in detail and leads to the Project Report with the detailed estimate. The Railway Board has asked zones to complete the traffic study before going ahead with an FLS.

Can a drone or LiDAR survey be used for a railway FLS?

Yes. Railway Board instructions for FLS and DPR consultants say tools such as LiDAR survey and satellite imagery should be used, and aerial LiDAR has been used for high-speed rail corridors. A drone or LiDAR survey tied to DGPS control and bench marks supplies the topography; pegging, pillars, soil investigation and river studies are still done on the ground.

What accuracy and control does a drone FLS need?

Ground control points measured with DGPS along the full corridor, alternating on both sides of the alignment, heights tied to bench marks referred to mean sea level, and independent checkpoints reported as RMSE in X, Y and Z. Vertical accuracy matters most because earthwork quantities come from ground and formation levels.

What deliverables come out of a railway FLS survey?

An orthomosaic in GeoTIFF, a terrain model and contours, a LAS or LAZ point cloud where LiDAR is flown, the detailed plan, longitudinal section and cross-sections in DWG, bridge and culvert site plans, land plans and GIS layers in SHP, and a control and accuracy report.
