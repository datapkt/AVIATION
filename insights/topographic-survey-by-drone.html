Topographic survey

Topographic and contour survey by drone: method, accuracy and deliverables

How a drone topographic survey is planned, tied to ground control and checked, how to choose a contour interval the data can support, and what you should receive at the end.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 25 November 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: a drone topographic survey flies overlapping photos or LiDAR over the site, ties them to DGPS ground control, and processes them into a bare-ground terrain model (DTM), contours, spot levels and an orthomosaic. Choose the contour interval from the proven vertical accuracy, not the other way round: a common legacy rule sets the interval at about three times the vertical RMSE[2][3]. Accept the survey only with an independent checkpoint report.

What a topographic survey records

A topographic survey describes the shape of the ground and the features on it, in a stated coordinate system and height datum. For design work the core outputs are the same whatever the instrument: ground levels, contours, breaklines along kerbs, drains, embankment toes and tops, and the position of built features such as roads, buildings, poles, culverts and boundary walls.

A drone changes how those levels are collected. Instead of a surveyor walking to each point, the drone captures the whole surface, and software extracts the ground from it. That gives dense, continuous coverage, but it also means the ground has to be visible to the sensor, and the result has to be proved against independent ground measurements.

When a drone survey fits, and when it doesn't

A drone is usually the efficient choice for:

  • Open or lightly vegetated land: agricultural plots, industrial and township layouts, solar plant sites, quarries and mine leases, landfill and stockpile yards.
  • Long corridors: highways, canals, railway alignments and transmission routes, where walking a total station along the full length is slow.
  • Unsafe or hard-to-reach ground: steep slopes, active earthworks, riverbanks and landslide scars.
  • Repeat surveys: monthly progress or volume checks, where the same flight plan is flown again.

A total station or DGPS is still needed for:

  • Ground control and checkpoints for the drone survey itself.
  • Hard detail the drone cannot see: invert levels of drains and culverts, ground under bridges and dense tree cover, inside buildings.
  • Millimetre work: structural setting-out, rail levels, benchmark levelling and as-built checks to tight tolerances.
  • Small sites where a day of ground survey is quicker than mobilising a flight.

Most infrastructure topography ends up as a combination of the two. Our guide to drone survey vs DGPS and total station sets out which method does which part.

Planning the flight: GSD, overlap and terrain following

Ground sample distance (GSD) is the size of one image pixel on the ground, and it limits how much detail can be measured. It is set by the camera and the height above ground. Decide the GSD from the accuracy and contour interval you need, then work back to the flying height with our GSD calculator. For example, the Indian Bureau of Mines SOP for drone surveys of mine leases asks for imagery at 5 cm GSD or better[4].

Overlap is how much each photo shares with the next one along the strip (forward) and with the neighbouring strip (side). Generous overlap is what lets the software match the same ground in many photos. The same IBM SOP sets a minimum of 80% forward and 70% side overlap[4], and software makers advise raising overlap further over forests, crops, sand and other uniform surfaces[5].

Terrain following matters on hilly or stepped sites. If the drone flies at one fixed altitude over a valley and a ridge, the GSD and overlap change across the site, and the high ground gets less overlap. Pix4D's acquisition guidance is that the camera should be kept as far as possible at a constant height over the terrain to hold the planned GSD[5]. In practice that means flying with a terrain model loaded into the flight planner, or splitting the site into blocks at different heights.

Two further checks belong in the plan: flight permissions for the site's airspace zone, and the light. Low sun casts long shadows that hide detail, while wind and haze blur images. Plan the flight window, not only the flight lines.

Ground control and checkpoints

Ground control points (GCPs) are marked targets measured with DGPS. They fix the drone model to the project coordinate system and correct its scale and height. Checkpoints are measured the same way but kept out of processing, so they can test the finished surface independently. RTK or PPK positioning on the drone reduces the number of GCPs needed; it does not remove the need for checkpoints.

  • Spread the control: around the boundary and through the middle of area sites, and in a zigzag on both sides of corridors. Clustered control leaves the far parts of the model unconstrained.
  • Tie to the project datum: GNSS gives ellipsoidal heights. Design levels in India are normally referred to mean sea level through a benchmark, so the survey must state how heights were reduced, using a geoid model or a local benchmark tie, and must agree with the levels the designers will use.
  • Use national references: the Survey of India operates a nationwide Continuously Operating Reference Stations (CORS) network of more than 1,000 stations, which the government describes as capable of centimetre-level positioning in real time[6]. It is a convenient way to bring control onto a common national framework.

How many GCPs, how far apart and how accuracy is proved are covered in detail in how accurate is a drone survey? This guide only summarises them.

Photogrammetry or LiDAR for vegetated sites

Photogrammetry builds the surface from what the camera sees. On bare ground that is the ground. Over crops, scrub and trees it is the top of the vegetation, and no amount of processing can recover ground that never appeared in any photo. Ground filtering can remove isolated trees and vehicles, but under continuous cover the "terrain" is really canopy.

LiDAR fires laser pulses that can return several echoes, the last often from the ground through gaps in the leaves. That is why LiDAR is the usual choice for terrain under vegetation, forest corridors, hill roads and landslide slopes. Many projects fly both: LiDAR for the terrain, and a camera for the orthomosaic and feature mapping.

The ASPRS standard reflects this difference by reporting accuracy separately for open ground (non-vegetated vertical accuracy, NVA) and for vegetated ground (VVA). NVA must meet the specification; VVA is reported as found[1]. Ask for both on any site with vegetation. Our LiDAR vs photogrammetry guide compares the two in field studies.

From images to a terrain model and contours

Processing follows a fixed chain. Images, camera positions and GCPs go into aerial triangulation, which solves where every photo was taken. Dense matching then builds a point cloud, from which a surface model (DSM) is made. Ground points are classified to give the terrain model (DTM). Contours, spot levels and CAD layers are drawn from the DTM, and the checkpoints test the result at the end.

Two steps decide whether the contours are usable. The first is ground classification: contours drawn from the DSM put trees, trucks and buildings in as humps. The second is breaklines: digitising kerbs, drain edges, bund tops and embankment toes so that contours bend sharply where the ground does, instead of being smoothed across a hard edge.

Choosing the contour interval

The contour interval should follow from the vertical accuracy the survey can prove. The current ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards, Edition 2 (2023), state plainly that they do not endorse contour interval, map scale or GSD as a way of expressing accuracy; accuracy is specified as a vertical RMSE[1]. The legacy rules still give a useful rule of thumb for picking an interval from that RMSE:

  • ASPRS 1990, Class 1: the limiting RMSE of contours is one-third of the contour interval, and spot heights one-sixth[2]. In other words, the interval is about three times the vertical RMSE, and spot levels should be twice as good as the contours.
  • NMAS (1947): no more than one tested point in ten may be in error by more than half the contour interval. Edition 2's comparison table puts the equivalent interval for its 10 cm vertical class at 30 cm under ASPRS 1990 Class 1 and about 33 cm under NMAS[3].
Proved vertical RMSE (open ground)Contour interval, ASPRS 1990 Class 1 (3 × RMSE)Spot level RMSE needed (interval ÷ 6)

10 cm30 cm5 cm

Read the table from left to right. If the checkpoint report proves 10 cm, a 30 cm interval is defensible; a 10 cm interval drawn from the same data looks detailed but claims precision the survey does not have. Where a project specification already fixes an interval, such as the highway DPR requirements covered in our highway DPR guide, work backwards to the RMSE needed and plan GSD and control to reach it.

QC and the accuracy report

The accuracy report is the evidence that the contours can be trusted. Under ASPRS Edition 2 it should be based on at least 30 checkpoints, surveyed at least twice as accurately as the product being tested, and the single headline figure is the RMSE; the old 95% confidence figure has been dropped[1]. A useful report includes:

  • the coordinate system, height datum and geoid or benchmark used;
  • a plan showing GCPs and checkpoints;
  • a table of checkpoint residuals in X, Y and Z, with horizontal and vertical RMSE;
  • vertical accuracy on open ground and, where relevant, under vegetation;
  • areas of low confidence, such as water, dense canopy or deep shadow, marked on the plan.

The detail of how residuals and RMSE are calculated is in the accuracy guide. Terms such as NVA, VVA and RMSE are defined in the drone survey glossary.

Common mistakes

  1. Contours from the DSM. Trees and structures appear as hills. Contours must come from a classified DTM.
  2. Ellipsoidal heights delivered as levels. GNSS heights not reduced to the project datum can differ from benchmark levels by tens of metres, and the error is not always obvious on a contour plan.
  3. An interval finer than the accuracy. Tight contours from loosely controlled data give a false sense of precision.
  4. No independent checkpoints. Residuals on the GCPs themselves only show how well the software fitted the control, not how accurate the map is.
  5. Clustered or one-sided control. Errors grow towards the parts of the site with no control, especially on long corridors.
  6. Photogrammetry under canopy. The terrain is the treetops. Use LiDAR or ground survey for those areas.
  7. Missing breaklines. Drains, kerbs and embankments get smoothed away, and designers have to redraw them.
  8. Water treated as ground. Ponds and rivers give poor or false surfaces; mark them and survey the bed separately if needed.

Frequently asked questions

What is a topographic survey by drone?

A drone topographic survey captures overlapping photos or LiDAR over a site, ties them to DGPS ground control, and processes them into a bare-ground terrain model, contours, spot levels, an orthomosaic and a topographic plan in the project coordinate system and height datum. Its accuracy is proved with independent checkpoints.

How do I choose the contour interval for a drone survey?

Choose it from the vertical accuracy the survey can prove. Under the legacy ASPRS 1990 Class 1 rule the contour interval is about three times the vertical RMSE, so a survey proved to 10 cm supports a 30 cm interval. The current ASPRS Edition 2 standard specifies accuracy as RMSE, not as a contour interval.

Can a drone contour survey map ground under trees?

Photogrammetry cannot: it maps the top of the vegetation where the ground is hidden. LiDAR pulses can reach the ground through gaps in the canopy, so LiDAR is the usual choice for vegetated sites, with ground survey for any areas neither sensor can see.

Does a drone topographic survey replace a total station or DGPS?

No. DGPS is needed for ground control and checkpoints, and a total station is still used for invert levels, ground under bridges and dense cover, and millimetre-level work. Most infrastructure surveys combine a drone for coverage with ground survey for control and hidden detail.

What deliverables should a drone topographic survey include?

Usually a DTM as GeoTIFF, contours and spot levels as DWG or DXF and SHP, an orthomosaic as GeoTIFF, a topographic plan of features, a point cloud as LAS or LAZ, and a survey report with the datum, GCP and checkpoint tables and RMSE in X, Y and Z.
