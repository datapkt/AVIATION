Bridge inspection by drone in India: what it can and cannot replace

A drone gives the bridge engineer close, repeatable views of piers, bearings, girders and the deck underside without an inspection unit or scaffolding. It does not replace hammer sounding, testing or the engineer's judgement. Here is where it fits under IRC guidelines and the Indian Railway Bridge Manual.

By the DATA PKT survey and inspection team · Published 25 November 2026 · 9 min read

Short answer: a drone replaces much of the access in a bridge inspection, not the inspection. It photographs the parts that otherwise need a bridge inspection unit, a boat or scaffolding, and turns them into a measurable 3D model and a located defect register. Hammer sounding, non-destructive testing, load tests, underwater and confined-space checks, and the condition assessment itself still need people on the structure and a qualified bridge engineer.

How bridges are inspected in India

Highway bridges: IRC guidelines and IBMS

The Indian Roads Congress guidance comes in layers. IRC:SP:35, published in 1990, contains the guidelines on inspection and maintenance of bridges and is written for the engineers who inspect them; IRC:SP:52, the Bridge Inspector's Reference Manual, is the companion for inspectors in the field[1]. The Manual for Highway Bridge Maintenance Inspection (IRC:SP:18) asks for every bridge to be inspected by a competent qualified engineer once a year, bridges in hilly terrain prone to slips twice (before and after the monsoon), and special inspections after unusual events such as an earthquake or the passage of an abnormal load. Major and special-type bridges should also be seen by a senior engineer[2].

MoRTH is now building a central record. Under the Indian Bridge Management System (IBMS), field engineers record inventory and condition on a mobile app, and a web dashboard gives agencies such as NHAI, NHIDCL, BRO and state PWDs access to the data. The 2026 drive covers about 1.7 lakh structures longer than six metres, including bridges, flyovers and culverts, and the ministry has said drones may be used for the hard-to-reach parts of bridges[3]. IBMS uses condition ratings and importance to prioritise repair, rehabilitation and maintenance[4]. The Indian Academy of Highway Engineers (IAHE) runs a course on bridge inspection, repair, maintenance management and health monitoring of structures for highway engineers[5].

Railway bridges: IRBM, BMS and RDSO BS-129

On Indian Railways, Chapter II of the Indian Railway Bridge Manual (IRBM) sets the schedule of bridge inspection by each grade of engineering official, and para 1107 lists the aspects an inspection must cover[6]. Pre-monsoon inspection is done by the SSE (P.Way) or SSE (Works) and post-monsoon inspection by the ADEN, and their paper bridge registers have been replaced by the online Bridge Management System (BMS)[7]. Underwater inspection of substructures follows IRBM para 1107(d): a routine or swim-by inspection once a year, a detailed inspection once in five years, and special inspections when needed[8]. Steel bridges also get periodic technical inspection by Bridge Inspectors, and in 2023 the Railway Board ordered intensive inspection of all open web and under-slung girder bridges by senior officers[6].

Drones already have a written place in this system. RDSO Report BS-129 (R1), May 2021, gave guidelines for drone-based bridge inspection. The Railway Board later noted that drone photography alone allows only an inference about condition, an incomplete inspection, and the guidelines were revised for complete inspection using photogrammetry and LiDAR, with the data held in a 3D bridge information model (BrIM) reached through BMS[9]. For railway work in general, see our guide to railway drone inspection.

What a drone does well on a bridge

  • Close views of the parts that need access: pier caps, bearings, girder soffits, diaphragms, expansion joints, drainage spouts and the deck underside. A camera on a gimbal that tilts upwards photographs the soffit without a bridge inspection unit on the deck or a boat underneath.
  • Defect imagery: cracks, spalling, exposed and corroded reinforcement, leaching, honeycombing, corrosion and section loss on steel, missing or visibly loose bolts and rivets, vegetation growth, and debris or erosion at the waterline. Flown a few metres from the surface with a zoom lens, the images can show fine cracks; the engineer decides what they mean.
  • A measurable 3D model: overlapping photographs are processed into a point cloud and textured mesh, tied to DGPS ground control, so each defect can be located on the structure, measured and compared at the next inspection. See photogrammetry for how the model is built.
  • Orthomosaics of the deck and elevations: a scaled, distortion-free image of the deck surface or a pier face that can be marked up like a drawing.
  • Thermal imagery, where it applies: on a concrete deck warmed by the sun, delaminated areas can show as warmer patches because the separation slows the flow of heat into the slab. Results depend on weather, time of day and surfacing, and every suspect area has to be confirmed by sounding. Our thermal survey page explains the camera and conditions.
  • Approaches and surroundings: embankments, guide bunds, river training works, the channel upstream and downstream, and encroachments, all in one flight.
  • Less disruption: fewer lane closures or traffic blocks for access, and fewer people working at height or over water.

What a drone cannot replace

  • Hands-on testing. IRC:SP:52 relies on touch and sound: tapping concrete and masonry with a hammer, checking rivets by striking one side and feeling the other, and tests for carbonation depth and chemical attack[1]. Non-destructive tests such as rebound hammer, ultrasonic pulse velocity, half-cell potential and cover measurement need contact with the surface.
  • Bearings in detail. A drone shows the bearing and its seat. It cannot by itself confirm whether a bearing is working as fixed or free, which SP:52 asks the inspector to establish first[1], or measure gaps and movement to the precision of a gauge.
  • Load tests and deflection. Load-carrying capacity comes from testing, instrumentation and analysis, not imagery.
  • Underwater substructure and scour. Below the waterline the work needs divers, special equipment or soundings[8][2].
  • Confined and GNSS-denied spaces. Inside box girders and closed cells there is no satellite signal, little light and no room to recover a drone safely. Under a wide deck, satellite positioning weakens or drops out. A person with a torch is usually the safer choice inside a box girder.
  • The assessment. Condition rating, cause of distress, load capacity and repair decisions belong to a qualified bridge engineer. Parts that could not be seen should be recorded as "not inspected", as SP:52 advises[1], rather than assumed sound.

How a drone bridge inspection is done

1. Plan

Start from the drawings, the last inspection report and the span arrangement, and fix the component numbering (spans, piers, bearings, faces) before anyone flies, so every photograph has an address. Check the airspace on Digital Sky and get the owner's permission: for a railway bridge, the division and a block or safe flying window; for a highway bridge, the authority and any traffic management. Note the river level, overhead lines and nearby buildings.

2. Safety

Each site gets a risk assessment, an exclusion zone, a spotter and an agreed abort plan. Hazards specific to bridges include gusting wind under the deck, birds and bee hives under girders, overhead power lines, and railway OHE, which a drone must keep well clear of.

3. Flying under and near the deck

Under the deck and beside tall piers the drone may lose satellite positioning and fall back on its vision sensors, which need light and surface texture. The pilot flies slowly and manually, keeps the aircraft in sight or works with an observer, and uses a compact drone with obstacle sensing and an upward-tilting camera. Image positions are taken from control on the structure, not from the drone's GNSS.

4. Image overlap for the 3D model

Each face is photographed in parallel strips at a constant distance, with enough forward and side overlap that every point appears in several photographs, plus oblique shots around corners and edges. Even light matters: shadow lines under a deck confuse both the software and the reviewer. DGPS ground control on the approaches and measured targets on the structure fix the scale and position. The drone survey glossary explains GSD, GCPs and related terms.

5. Defect logging

Every defect is logged with its component, its position on that component (span, face, distance from a bearing or joint), type, approximate size, photo reference and a note on change since the last inspection. The register is a tool for the inspecting engineer, not a substitute for the engineer's rating.

Deliverables

  • Inspection imagery: full-resolution photographs and video, organised by span and component, each with its location.
  • Defect register: a spreadsheet and GIS layer listing each defect with component, position, type, size, photo reference and priority for the engineer's review.
  • 3D model: point cloud and textured mesh of the bridge, checked against DGPS checkpoints.
  • Orthomosaics of the deck and of each elevation, scaled for mark-up.
  • Thermal images, where used, with the time, weather and surface conditions recorded.
  • Coverage note: what was flown, what was not inspected and why.

Checklist for bridge owners and consultants

  1. State the format the results must feed: IBMS, an IRC inspection proforma, the railway BMS, or RDSO's BrIM guidelines.
  2. Share the drawings, span arrangement and last inspection report.
  3. Agree the component numbering before the flight.
  4. Name the inspecting engineer; the drone team supports that engineer.
  5. Plan hammer sounding, NDT and underwater inspection separately.
  6. Confirm permissions, blocks or traffic arrangements and the flying window.
  7. Ask for a coverage map and a "not inspected" list.
  8. Ask for every defect to be located on a component and linked to a photograph.
  9. Ask how the 3D model was checked against ground control.
  10. Agree file formats and that the data belongs to you.

Frequently asked questions

Can a drone replace a bridge inspection in India?

No. A drone replaces much of the access: it photographs piers, bearings, girders and the deck underside without an inspection unit, boat or scaffolding, and builds a measurable 3D model. Hammer sounding, non-destructive tests, load tests, underwater checks and the condition assessment still need people on the structure and a qualified bridge engineer.

How often must highway bridges be inspected under IRC guidelines?

IRC:SP:18 asks for every bridge to be inspected by a competent qualified engineer once a year, bridges in hilly terrain prone to slips twice (before and after the monsoon), and special inspections after events such as an earthquake or an abnormal load. IRC:SP:35 (1990) sets out the guidelines for inspection and maintenance of bridges.

Does Indian Railways allow drone inspection of bridges?

Yes. RDSO Report BS-129 (R1), issued in May 2021, gives guidelines for drone-based bridge inspection. The Railway Board noted that photography alone is an incomplete inspection, and the guidelines were revised to use photogrammetry and LiDAR, with the data held in a 3D bridge model reached through the Bridge Management System.

Can a drone fly under a bridge deck?

Yes, with care. Under the deck satellite positioning weakens or drops out, so the pilot flies slowly and manually with vision sensors, an upward-tilting camera and an observer. Image positions come from control points on the structure. Inside box girders and other confined spaces a person is usually the safer choice.

What should a drone bridge inspection deliver?

Full-resolution imagery organised by span and component, a defect register giving each defect's component, position, type, size and photo reference, a 3D model checked against DGPS checkpoints, orthomosaics of the deck and elevations, thermal images where used, and a note of what was not inspected.
