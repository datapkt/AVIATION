LiDAR vs photogrammetry: which drone survey do you need?

LiDAR or photogrammetry? How each works, accuracy, vegetation, cost and deliverables compared, with the right choice for highways, railways, power lines, mines and hills.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 27 October 2026 · Updated 13 November 2026 · 12 min read

Short answer: on open, bare ground the two are close: both reach about 10–15 cm height accuracy with good control, and photogrammetry gives the sharper map for less money. Once the ground is hidden by vegetation, the gap opens fast. In one study photogrammetry's error tripled to about 36 cm while LiDAR stayed near 11 cm; under dense forest, photogrammetric terrain was off by 2.1 m. Choose LiDAR for vegetation, forests, wires and long corridors; photogrammetry for open sites, mines and stockpiles; and both on most highway and railway projects.

How each sensor sees the ground

Photogrammetry is passive. The drone takes hundreds of overlapping photos and software finds the same feature in several of them, then works out its 3D position. Every point is something the camera saw: the top of a tree, a roof, a road. If nothing in the photos shows the ground, there is no ground in the model.

LiDAR is active. The scanner fires hundreds of thousands of laser pulses a second and times each echo. A single pulse can return several echoes: the first from leaves, later ones from branches, the last from the ground through a gap. A DJI Zenmuse L2 records up to 5 echoes per pulse, the newer L3 up to 16 and a RIEGL VUX-120-23 up to 32[12][13][15]. Software then keeps the last echoes and builds a bare-earth terrain model.

That one difference, seeing versus measuring through gaps, explains almost every result below.

Accuracy: on open ground it's a draw

Two independent studies flew drone LiDAR and drone photogrammetry over the same ground and checked both against RTK survey points:

  • On short grass (under 20 cm), LiDAR reached 10.9 cm RMSE and photogrammetry at 2 cm GSD 13.9 cm, against 193 checkpoints[1].
  • On the bare crest of a river levee, LiDAR reached 9.7 cm and RTK photogrammetry 14.7 cm[2].
  • Where the camera sees the ground clearly, photogrammetry can even beat LiDAR: about 1.5 cm in one leaf-off forest test[3].

So for a bare, open site, a well-controlled photogrammetry survey is accurate enough for most engineering work, and cheaper.

Add vegetation and the gap opens

As plants grow taller, the camera maps their tops and the "ground" rises with them. LiDAR's last echoes keep finding the real surface:

  • As vegetation grew from about 20 cm to over 60 cm, photogrammetry error rose from 24 → 27 → 36 cm. LiDAR held at about 11 cm[1].
  • Under dense shrubs below 1 m, unfiltered terrain error was 41 cm for photogrammetry and 31 cm for LiDAR. Good ground filtering cut both to about 17–18 cm[2]: processing skill matters as much as the sensor.

Under forest canopy, it isn't close

  • In dense tropical forest, only 0.01% of photogrammetry points fell within 1 m of the ground, against 3% of LiDAR points. The photogrammetric terrain model was off by 2.1 m RMSE[4].
  • In dense young forest, photogrammetry saw only 80.7% of the ground. LiDAR covered nearly all of it, and 89% of its ground points came from later echoes, not the first[3].

This is why hill roads, landslide slopes, forest corridors and transmission routes through the Northeast and the Himalayas are LiDAR jobs. A slope stability model built on a canopy surface puts the slip surface in the wrong place.

Wires, towers and overhead equipment

  • Photos taken along a power line cannot match the wire, because the conductor runs parallel to the software's search line. Workarounds need cross-strip photos and manual stereo work, and reach only about ±0.5 m[5].
  • Drone LiDAR at about 35 points/m² measured conductor-to-vegetation clearance on 220 kV lines to decimetre accuracy, automatically[6].

The same principle applies to railway overhead equipment, catenary and signal posts: if the deliverable includes thin structures in 3D, fly LiDAR.

Ground control: where photogrammetry projects go wrong

Photogrammetry needs ground control points (GCPs) to fix scale and height. Its height error is typically about 2.5 times its horizontal error[7], and on long, narrow corridors the layout of GCPs decides the result:

  • Area surveys: below 1 GCP per 100 photos, accuracy falls quickly. About 4 per 100 photos gave the best height accuracy, about 1.5× GSD[7].
  • Corridors: at least 7 GCPs were needed to get under 10 cm on the 2.1 km road[8].
  • LiDAR's position comes from GNSS and an inertial unit on the drone. Checkpoints are still used, mainly to confirm that overlapping strips fit together[10].

"Survey-grade without GCPs" claims from sensor makers (3 cm horizontal / 5 cm vertical for P1, 4 cm / 5 cm for L2) are measured in ideal conditions[12][14]. On a real project, plan checkpoints either way.

Light, surfaces and weather

  • Light: photogrammetry needs daylight and shadows that don't hide detail. LiDAR is an active sensor and works at night, but strong sun and dark surfaces shorten its range. The L2 reaches 450 m on a 50%-reflective target in the dark but 250 m on a 10%-reflective target in 100,000-lux sunlight[12].
  • Texture: photogrammetry needs visual texture to match. Snow, ice, still water, uniform sand and fresh black asphalt are classic weak spots. LiDAR doesn't need texture, but neither sensor sees through water.
  • Flight height: in India's green zone, drones fly without permission up to 400 ft (about 120 m)[16]. Long-range LiDAR is rated for far higher flights, so in India its range mostly buys wider swaths and better penetration, not altitude.

Where photogrammetry is the right choice

  • The sharpest true-colour map. A 45 MP full-frame mapping camera (P1) beats the smaller cameras built into LiDAR units for orthomosaic detail[12][14].
  • Mines and stockpiles. The Indian Bureau of Mines drone SOP asks for an orthomosaic at 5 cm GSD or better, at least 5 GCPs per km² and an RMSE report[11]. Photogrammetry meets that naturally. Stockpile volumes came within 4–6% at 0.8 cm GSD in a 2025 study[17].
  • Open, bare sites: graded land, quarries, urban and cadastral mapping, construction progress.
  • Budget. A survey LiDAR unit such as the L2 cost about USD 13,600–14,700 at launch, and high-end scanners cost far more. A mapping camera is cheaper, and so is each repeat flight.

How the accuracy should be reported

Ask for a checkpoint report whichever sensor you choose. The ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards, Edition 2 (2023), are the current reference[9]:

  • At least 30 checkpoints (up to 120 on large projects), surveyed at least twice as accurately as the product.
  • One headline number: RMSE. The old "95% confidence" figure is gone.
  • Accuracy on open ground (NVA) must meet the spec. Accuracy under vegetation (VVA) is reported, so you can see how each sensor did where it matters.

The USGS quality levels give a simple density and accuracy yardstick for LiDAR terrain[10]:

Quality levelPoint densityHeight accuracy, open ground (RMSE)

These levels were written for aircraft surveys. Drone LiDAR at 35–200 points/m² is far denser.

What's new in 2025–2026

  • DJI Zenmuse L3 (late 2025): a 1535 nm laser with a 0.25 mrad beam, up to 16 echoes per pulse and two 100 MP cameras, rated at 3 cm vertical / 4 cm horizontal accuracy at 120 m[13]. Its cameras narrow the map-quality gap with photogrammetry.
  • RIEGL VUX-120-23: up to 2 million measurements a second, with forward, backward and nadir scanning for steep cuttings, poles and facades[15].
  • Gaussian splatting: a newer way to build 3D models from photos, now in DJI Terra 5.0 (2025). In one 2026 study, point measurements on splat models reached 1–2 cm, and thin structures came out better than on meshes[18]. It is promising for visual models, but it doesn't solve the problem of ground hidden under vegetation.

How we decide at DATA PKT

We fly both, and on most highway, railway and hill-road projects we combine them: LiDAR for the terrain and anything thin, and a camera for the map. Before quoting, we look at three things: how much of the ground is hidden, whether the deliverable needs wires or vertical faces, and how long and narrow the corridor is. Then we plan ground control to match. Send us the KML and the outputs you need, and we'll tell you which method, or which mix, gives the accuracy you need at the lowest cost.

Frequently asked questions

Is LiDAR more accurate than photogrammetry?

On open, bare ground they are close: field studies found about 10–11 cm height error for drone LiDAR and 14–15 cm for photogrammetry with good ground control. Under vegetation over 60 cm, photogrammetry error rose to about 36 cm while LiDAR stayed near 11 cm, and under dense forest photogrammetric terrain was off by about 2 m.

Can photogrammetry see the ground under trees?

Only where the ground is visible between trees. In dense tropical forest only about 0.01% of photogrammetry points fell within 1 m of the ground, against about 3% for LiDAR, because laser pulses return several echoes and the last one often comes from the ground.

Which is cheaper, LiDAR or photogrammetry?

Photogrammetry is usually more economical: the camera costs less and repeat flights are cheap. A survey LiDAR such as the DJI Zenmuse L2 cost about USD 13,600–14,700 at launch. LiDAR earns its cost where the ground is hidden, on long corridors and where wires or thin structures must be mapped.

How many GCPs does a drone photogrammetry survey need?

For area surveys, research found accuracy falls quickly below 1 GCP per 100 photos and was best at about 4 per 100. On a 2.1 km road, at least 7 GCPs in a zigzag on both sides (about 5 per km) were needed to get under 10 cm; GCPs on one side only gave errors up to 93 cm.

Can I get a colour map from LiDAR?

Yes. Most survey LiDAR units carry a camera: 20 MP on the DJI L2 and two 100 MP cameras on the L3, so the point cloud can be coloured and an orthomosaic made from the same flight. A dedicated full-frame mapping camera still gives the sharpest orthomosaic.

Which is better for power lines and railway OHE?

LiDAR. Photogrammetry fails to match wires that run along the flight strip, while LiDAR records each pulse that hits a conductor as a 3D point, so sag and vegetation clearance can be measured to decimetre accuracy.
