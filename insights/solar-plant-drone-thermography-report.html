Solar plant drone thermography: what the inspection report should include

A thermal drone flight is only as useful as the report that comes out of it. Use this checklist to plan an inspection and judge whether a report is good enough to act on.

By the DATA PKT survey and GIS engineering team · Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 24 November 2026

Short answer: a good solar thermography report lists every defect with its type, exact location (block, string, module), temperature difference and severity, backed by a thermal and a visual image of each one, a plant map, and a record of the conditions during the flight. The widely used standard for aerial thermography of PV plants is IEC TS 62446-3.

Flight conditions that matter

Thermal images only show faults clearly when the modules are working hard and conditions are stable. IEC TS 62446-3 sets a minimum irradiance of 600 W/m² in the plane of the modules, with clear sky and stable conditions. In practice this means flying around the middle of the day on clear days with the plant running normally.

  • Use a radiometric thermal camera, which records a temperature for every pixel.
  • Record irradiance, ambient temperature, wind and cloud cover during the flight.
  • Fly at a height and angle that give enough thermal resolution on each module and avoid sun reflections.

Checklist: what the report should contain

  1. Plant details, date, time and the weather and irradiance during the flight.
  2. Equipment used, including the thermal camera and whether it is radiometric.
  3. A plant map or orthomosaic with every defect marked.
  4. For each defect: type, location (block, inverter, string, module), temperature difference against normal modules, and severity.
  5. A thermal image and a matching visual image of each defect.
  6. A summary table by defect type and severity, with recommended action.
  7. Data files your O&M team can use, such as a spreadsheet and GIS layers.

What the report is used for

Owners and O&M teams use the report to prioritise repairs, support warranty claims with module makers, plan cleaning and vegetation control, and compare plant health year on year. A repeat flight after repairs confirms the faults are fixed.

Frequently asked questions

What should a solar plant drone thermography report include?

It should record plant details, date, time, weather and irradiance; the equipment used and whether the camera is radiometric; a plant map or orthomosaic with every defect marked; each defect's type, location, temperature difference and severity with matching thermal and visual images; a summary table with recommended actions; and spreadsheet and GIS files for the O&M team.

Which standard applies to drone thermography of solar PV plants?

The widely used standard for aerial thermography of PV plants is IEC TS 62446-3. It sets a minimum irradiance of 600 W/m² in the plane of the modules, with clear sky and stable conditions, so that faults show clearly in the thermal images.

What defects can a thermal drone inspection find on a solar plant?

Typical findings are single-cell hot spots, multi-cell or patchwork heating linked to cell degradation or PID, hot substrings from active or failed bypass diodes, whole modules warm from disconnection or open circuit, strings off due to fuse or connector faults, junction box and connector heating, and soiling or shading.

When should a solar thermography drone flight be done?

In general practice, around the middle of the day on clear days with the plant running normally, so the modules are working hard and conditions are stable. A radiometric thermal camera should be used, and irradiance, ambient temperature, wind and cloud cover recorded during the flight.

Who analyses your solar thermography data?

Our thermal team includes a thermographer certified to ISO 9712 Level 2, and our in-house GIS team processes the survey data. ISO 9712 is the international standard for qualifying and certifying non-destructive testing personnel, a scheme that includes infrared thermography.
