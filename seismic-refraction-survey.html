Seismic refraction survey and tomography (SRT)

See below the surface without digging: DATA PKT runs seismic refraction surveys to find soil thickness, depth to rock and weak zones for slopes, roads, tunnels, dams and foundations, positioned by DGPS and read together with drone LiDAR and geology.

FindsSoil thickness, depth to weathered and fresh rock, weak or saturated zones
Typical depthAbout 20–30 m with a 24-channel spread
Used forLandslides and slopes, roads, tunnels, dams, bridges, foundations
Recent project66 profiles along a road corridor in Assam

In short: seismic refraction measures how fast sound travels through the ground. Sound moves faster in harder material, so the arrival times recorded by a line of geophones show where loose soil ends, where rock begins and where the ground is weak. Tomographic inversion turns those times into a velocity section along each profile, which engineers use to set the depth of slip surfaces, soil nails, anchors and foundations.

How a seismic refraction survey works

A line of 24 geophones is laid along the profile, typically 3–5 m apart. A sledgehammer is struck on a steel plate at several shot points along and beyond the line, and the seismograph records the waves at every geophone. Each shot is repeated and stacked to lift the signal above traffic and wind noise. Waves that refract along faster layers reach distant geophones first; the pattern of first-arrival times reveals each layer's depth and velocity.

What the velocities tell you

P-wave velocity rises as the ground gets harder and less fractured. The ranges below are typical guides only; every site is calibrated against geology and, where available, boreholes.

Typical P-wave velocityWhat it usually meansWhy it matters
Below about 800 m/sDry soil, fill, loose colluviumMaterial most likely to slide; thickness sets nail length and excavation
About 800–1,500 m/sCompact or saturated soil, highly weathered rockOften the zone of shallow slides after rain; water table indicator
About 1,500–2,500 m/sWeathered or fractured rockPossible slip zones; rock that can often be ripped
Above about 2,500 m/sFresh, competent rockAnchor bond zone, foundation level, tunnel rock

Where we use it

  • Landslides and slopes: thickness of unstable cover and depth of possible slip surfaces. See landslide and slope stability.
  • Roads and railways in hills: cut-slope design, depth to rock along the alignment, rippability for excavation.
  • Tunnels and portals: rock quality and weak zones at portals and shallow sections.
  • Dams, weirs and barrages: depth to bedrock along the axis and at abutments.
  • Bridges, substations and buildings: foundation level between boreholes.

Why combine it with drone LiDAR

Every geophone and shot point is surveyed by DGPS, and the ground elevations along the profile come from our drone LiDAR terrain model. The tomogram therefore sits exactly on the true ground surface and plots directly on the LiDAR cross-sections, so surface shape and subsurface layers are read together in one ground model. That is how we work on LiDAR and slope projects.

Frequently asked questions

What is a seismic refraction survey?

A geophysical survey that measures how fast sound waves travel through the ground. A hammer or other source sends waves into the ground and a line of geophones records them. Waves travel faster in harder material, so the arrival times show the thickness of soil, the depth to weathered and fresh rock, and weak zones, without digging.

What is the difference between seismic refraction and seismic refraction tomography (SRT)?

Classic refraction interpretation assumes a few flat layers. Tomography divides the ground into many small cells and solves for the velocity of each, so it shows gradual changes, dipping layers and local weak zones. That makes it better suited to slopes and variable ground.

How deep can seismic refraction see?

Roughly one-third to one-fifth of the spread length. A 24-channel spread with geophones 3 to 5 m apart typically investigates about 20 to 30 m, which covers most slope, road and foundation problems. Longer spreads or stronger sources reach deeper.

Does seismic refraction replace boreholes?

No. It covers the ground between and beyond boreholes, at far lower cost per metre, and shows where boreholes are most needed. Boreholes then give samples, laboratory strength and a check on the velocity model. The two work best together.

When does seismic refraction not work well?

Where a hard layer lies above a softer one (a velocity inversion), the softer layer can be missed. Heavy traffic or machinery adds noise. We plan spreads, shot timing and stacking around these limits, and check results against geology and boreholes.

What do you deliver?

SEG-2 field data, first-break picks, a velocity tomogram for each profile with ray coverage and RMS error, an interpreted section showing soil, weathered and fresh rock and weak zones, depth to competent strata, and a location map in your coordinate system.
