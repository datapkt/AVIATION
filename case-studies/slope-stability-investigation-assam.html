Case study · Landslide and slope stability

Slope stability investigation of a 66 km road corridor in Assam

For a private client, DATA PKT investigated the road-side slopes of a ~66 km hill road corridor in Assam with drone LiDAR, seismic refraction tomography and geological mapping, and delivered a stability report that ranked every stretch and gave the inputs to design its protection.

Published 2 November 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026

LocationRoad corridor in Assam, Northeast India
LengthAbout 66 km of road-side cut and fill slopes
ClientPrivate client (name withheld)
DurationAbout 9 weeks, with about 3 weeks in the field

In short: we flew drone LiDAR along the full ~66 km corridor, shot 66 seismic refraction profiles (about one per kilometre), mapped the geology in detail at 7 critical locations (2.12 km), and combined all three in one ground model. The stability analysis gave a factor of safety for each critical section in dry, monsoon-saturated and earthquake conditions, and every stretch was ranked by hazard with remedial measures and protection design inputs.

The challenge

The client needed to know which slopes along the corridor were most likely to fail, how, and what it would take to hold them, so that slope stabilisation and rockfall protection could be selected and dimensioned for the critical stretches. The corridor is a hard place to answer that question:

  • Very heavy monsoon rain, of the order of 2,500–3,500 mm a year, which saturates the slopes
  • Seismic Zone V under IS 1893, the highest seismic zone in India
  • Dense vegetation covering the slopes, hiding the ground from photographs
  • Soft alluvial soils and weathered sedimentary rock in the cut sections
  • Failures mainly through rain-induced shallow slides, erosion and toe scour, and structurally controlled rock failures
  • A narrow, live road: every field crew had to work around traffic

Our approach: three data sets, one ground model

No single method can characterise a slope. We planned three independent investigations, each answering its own question, and brought them together for the analysis:

  • Drone LiDAR showed what the ground surface looks like: slope height and angle, benches, cracks, scarps, drainage paths and changes at the road edge, under the vegetation.
  • Seismic refraction showed what lies below: the thickness of loose cover, the depth to weathered and fresh rock, and low-velocity zones that are weak or saturated.
  • Geological mapping showed how the material behaves: rock mass quality, joint orientations, possible failure mechanisms and shear strength.
  • The stability analysis put the three together: geometry from LiDAR, layering from SRT and strength from geology gave a factor of safety for each critical section.

Seismic profiles were placed where they gave the most information: high cuts and embankments, visible distress, scour-prone sections beside water, and the seven geology locations. Their exact positions were fixed after reconnaissance and a quick-look LiDAR model, and every profile was surveyed by DGPS so the tomograms plot directly on the LiDAR cross-sections.

How a corridor investigation like this is planned

LiDAR flight design

  • Point density is set by the flight. For one strip, point density is roughly the sensor's pulse rate divided by (ground speed × swath width), and swath width is 2 × height × tan(half the scan angle). Flying lower or slower, or adding strips, raises density. Height, line count and density in a specification such as the one below are traded off against each other in this way.
  • Ground-point density decides the bare-earth model. Under dense canopy only a fraction of pulses reach the ground, so what matters for the DTM is the density of ground points after classification, not total density.
  • Scan angle and multiple returns. A narrower usable scan angle gives more near-vertical pulses that pass through gaps in the canopy, and recording multiple returns per pulse lets the last returns reach the ground.
  • Terrain following keeps the height above ground near constant on a road that climbs and falls through hill sections.
  • IMU and trajectory. Flights begin and end with an alignment manoeuvre (such as a figure-of-eight) to settle the inertial solution, and the base station logs for the whole flight for the PPK trajectory.
  • Boresight calibration fixes the small angles between the laser scanner and the IMU, so that overlapping strips agree.

Control design for a hill corridor

  • Two layers of control. Static GNSS pillars, tied to Survey of India or CORS, form the backbone; ground control points and check points are measured from them.
  • Where LiDAR GCPs go. On LiDAR, GCPs are used mainly to remove vertical bias between strips and flights and to check the boresight, so they belong on flat, open, hard surfaces (road pavement, culvert tops, clearings) rather than on slopes, where a small horizontal error turns into a large vertical one.
  • Check points held out. Check points are kept out of every adjustment and spread along the full length, with a separate set under vegetation for the vegetated vertical accuracy (VVA) test.
  • One frame for every discipline. Geophones, shot points and geology stations are positioned from the same network as the LiDAR, so tomograms and mapped joints plot directly on the LiDAR sections, as they did on this corridor.

How each stream was processed

  1. LiDAR: raw scans and GNSS/IMU data → PPK trajectory from the base station → point cloud and strip adjustment → check against ground control and classification → DTM, DSM and contours → sections, slope maps, 3D models and KMZ.
  2. Seismic refraction: SEG-2 shot records → geometry and elevations from LiDAR → first-break picking → delay-time starting model → tomographic inversion → velocity section and interpretation.
  3. Geology: field sheets and photographs → discontinuity database → stereonets and kinematics → RMR, SMR and GSI → Hoek–Brown c′ and φ′ → stability input parameters.

From point cloud and tomogram to stability model

LiDAR classification under canopy

  • Point clouds are classified to the ASPRS LAS 1.4 classes: ground (2), low, medium and high vegetation (3, 4, 5), buildings (6), low noise (7), water (9) and high noise (18).
  • Ground is extracted with an automatic filter (progressive TIN densification and cloth simulation are common choices) and edited by hand on steep cuts, where automatic filters tend to clip the crest and toe of a slope or leave low vegetation in as ground.
  • Where canopy leaves too few ground returns for the DTM to be trusted, those areas are flagged, so that stability sections are not drawn through interpolated ground.

Building the stability model

  • Sections. Critical sections are cut from the DTM at the spacing given in the deliverables below, each carrying the LiDAR surface, the SRT velocity layers and the mapped discontinuities.
  • Layer boundaries from velocity. P-wave velocity ranges are assigned to materials (loose cover, weathered rock, fresher rock) and cross-checked against exposures at the geology locations.
  • Strength. Hoek–Brown parameters from GSI and field strength are converted to equivalent Mohr–Coulomb c′ and φ′ over the slope stress range, and joint strength is taken from Barton–Bandis.
  • Load cases. Limit equilibrium analyses (Bishop, Spencer, Morgenstern–Price) are run for dry or normal conditions; for monsoon-saturated conditions, with pore pressure set by a raised phreatic surface or a pore-pressure ratio (ru); and for pseudo-static seismic conditions, with a horizontal seismic coefficient derived from the zone factor for the site under IS 1893 (Part 1).
  • Rock slopes. Planar and wedge checks come from kinematic analysis of the mapped joint sets on stereonets.
  • Hazard ranking. A Low, Medium, High or Critical ranking typically weighs the factor of safety in the governing case, the failure mechanism, observed distress and the consequence of failure for the road.

Execution and schedule

The work took about nine weeks from the work order. The field window was about three weeks, with the LiDAR, geology and seismic crews working in parallel in the post-monsoon dry season.

A typical field day ran from a 06:30 toolbox talk (safety, traffic, weather and equipment check), through field work by three crews from 07:00 to 16:30 (the LiDAR crew flew in the calm morning air; the seismic crew shot in gaps in the traffic), to data download, dual backup and quality checks in the evening. The client received a daily progress report: chainage covered, profiles done, issues and the next day's plan.

How accuracy is reported, and the limits of each method

LiDAR accuracy is reported to the ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023), the standard named in the specification above. Edition 2 reports RMSE only, without confidence-level figures, needs at least 30 checkpoints for a product assessment, and includes the checkpoint survey's own error in the reported figure. Non-vegetated vertical accuracy (NVA) is the pass or fail measure; vegetated vertical accuracy (VVA) is reported without a pass or fail threshold.

Slope geometry (height, angle, bench widths) depends on relative accuracy within each section. Plotting tomograms, joints and LiDAR together depends on all three being in the same absolute frame. Both need checking.

What each method cannot do, and how that is handled

  • LiDAR ground under dense canopy. Few pulses reach the ground, so ground-point density is checked, low-confidence areas are flagged and VVA is reported separately from open ground.
  • Filters clipping crests and toes. Automatic ground filters treat sharp breaks in slope as objects, so ground on cut slopes is edited by hand.
  • Velocity inversion and hidden layers. Seismic refraction needs velocity to increase with depth and layers thick enough to return first arrivals (see ASTM D5777, Standard Guide for Using the Seismic Refraction Method for Subsurface Investigation). A slower layer beneath a faster one, or a thin layer, can be missed, so interpretation is cross-checked with geology and exposures, and weak zones are read as low-velocity anomalies in the tomogram rather than as layers.
  • Resolution falls with depth. Fewer rays reach the deeper parts of a section, which is why ray coverage is delivered with each tomogram and interpretation is limited to covered zones.
  • Traffic noise on a live road. Shots are taken in traffic gaps, stacked, and repeated when noisy.
  • Rock-mass strength from surface mapping. Joints at depth may differ from those on the face, which is why strength is best carried into the analysis as a range rather than a single value.

Safety and environment

  • Roadside work-zone traffic management to IRC:SP:55: cones, signboards, flagmen and reflective jackets at every seismic spread and drone take-off point
  • Drone operations under the Drone Rules, 2021 with Digital Sky permissions; no flying in rain, wind over 10 m/s, poor visibility or over crowds
  • Rock-face mapping only after a check for loose blocks; helmets at all times; an observer watching for rockfall; no climbing without rope access
  • Hammer-source safety: gloves, goggles, ear protection and a clearance zone
  • No tree felling; vegetation cleared by hand only along seismic lines; all waste taken back

Why it worked

  • One team, three disciplines: survey, geophysics and geology planned together and positioned on the same DGPS control, so every data set lines up.
  • LiDAR first, then the ground: a quick-look LiDAR model guided where the seismic lines went, putting effort where the risk was.
  • Built for the design that follows: every deliverable was chosen for the protection design it feeds, not for its own sake.
  • Corridor scale, short field window: parallel crews kept about 66 km of field work to about three weeks.

Getting the best results

  • Plan LiDAR for ground-point density under the canopy, not total point density.
  • Put LiDAR check points on open, flat, hard ground for NVA, and a separate set under vegetation for VVA.
  • Fly the LiDAR first and use a quick-look DTM to place the seismic lines.
  • Survey every geophone and shot point on the same control as the LiDAR, so geophysics and geometry line up without manual shifting.
  • Read seismic velocities with the geology alongside; refraction alone can miss a weak layer beneath a stronger one.
  • Carry strength as a range and test the sensitivity of the factor of safety to it, rather than relying on one "best estimate".
  • Run the saturated case for every critical section in a heavy-monsoon region; it is often the case that governs.
  • Deliver ray coverage and misfit with every tomogram, so the designer knows how far to trust each part of it.

Frequently asked questions

What did DATA PKT do on the Assam corridor?

We investigated the road-side cut and fill slopes along about 66 km of road in Assam: a drone LiDAR survey of the full corridor, 66 seismic refraction tomography profiles (about one per kilometre), detailed geological mapping at 7 critical locations totalling 2.12 km, and a slope stability analysis with stretch-wise hazard ranking and remedial recommendations.

Why were three methods combined?

Each answers a different question. LiDAR shows the shape of the slope, seismic refraction shows what lies below the surface, and geological mapping shows how the material behaves. Combined in one ground model, they give a factor of safety for each critical section that none of them could give alone.

How long did the investigation take?

About nine weeks from the work order to the final report, with about three weeks in the field. The LiDAR, seismic and geology crews worked in parallel to keep the field window short.

Why is this corridor difficult?

The region gets very heavy monsoon rain, lies in seismic Zone V under IS 1893, and has dense vegetation, soft alluvial soils and weathered sedimentary rock in the cuts. Slopes fail mainly through rain-induced shallow slides, erosion and toe scour, and failures controlled by rock joints.

How were the results used?

The report gave the slope geometry, the thickness of unstable layers, shear strength parameters and failure mechanisms needed to select and dimension flexible high-tensile steel protection systems such as soil-nailed mesh, rockfall drapes and barriers, together with a priority list of where to act first.

Can drone LiDAR map the ground under dense forest?

Partly. Some laser pulses pass through gaps in the canopy and return from the ground, and multiple-return sensors record them. How well the ground is mapped depends on how many ground returns remain after classification, so a good survey reports ground-point density and vegetated vertical accuracy separately from results on open ground.

What can seismic refraction not tell you about a slope?

Refraction relies on velocity increasing with depth, so it can miss a slower, weaker layer lying beneath a faster one, or a layer too thin to show in the first arrivals, and its resolution falls with depth. It is strongest when read together with geological mapping and, where available, boreholes.
