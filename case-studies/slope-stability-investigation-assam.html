Case study · Landslide and slope stability

Slope stability investigation of a 66 km road corridor in Assam

For a private client, DATA PKT investigated the road-side slopes of a ~66 km hill road corridor in Assam with drone LiDAR, seismic refraction tomography and geological mapping, and delivered a stability report that ranked every stretch and gave the inputs to design its protection.

LocationRoad corridor in Assam, Northeast India
LengthAbout 66 km of road-side cut and fill slopes
ClientPrivate client (name withheld)
DurationAbout 9 weeks, with about 3 weeks in the field

In short: we flew drone LiDAR along the full ~66 km corridor, shot 66 seismic refraction profiles (about one per kilometre), mapped the geology in detail at 7 critical locations (2.12 km), and combined all three in one ground model. The stability analysis gave a factor of safety for each critical section in dry, monsoon-saturated and earthquake conditions, and every stretch was ranked by hazard with remedial measures and protection design inputs.

The challenge

The client needed to know which slopes along the corridor were most likely to fail, how, and what it would take to hold them, so that slope stabilisation and rockfall protection could be selected and dimensioned for the critical stretches. The corridor is a hard place to answer that question:

  • Very heavy monsoon rain, of the order of 2,500–3,500 mm a year, which saturates the slopes
  • Seismic Zone V under IS 1893, the highest seismic zone in India
  • Dense vegetation covering the slopes, hiding the ground from photographs
  • Soft alluvial soils and weathered sedimentary rock in the cut sections
  • Failures mainly through rain-induced shallow slides, erosion and toe scour, and structurally controlled rock failures
  • A narrow, live road: every field crew had to work around traffic

Our approach: three data sets, one ground model

No single method can characterise a slope. We planned three independent investigations, each answering its own question, and brought them together for the analysis:

  • Drone LiDAR showed what the ground surface looks like: slope height and angle, benches, cracks, scarps, drainage paths and changes at the road edge, under the vegetation.
  • Seismic refraction showed what lies below: the thickness of loose cover, the depth to weathered and fresh rock, and low-velocity zones that are weak or saturated.
  • Geological mapping showed how the material behaves: rock mass quality, joint orientations, possible failure mechanisms and shear strength.
  • The stability analysis put the three together: geometry from LiDAR, layering from SRT and strength from geology gave a factor of safety for each critical section.

Seismic profiles were placed where they gave the most information: high cuts and embankments, visible distress, scour-prone sections beside water, and the seven geology locations. Their exact positions were fixed after reconnaissance and a quick-look LiDAR model, and every profile was surveyed by DGPS so the tomograms plot directly on the LiDAR cross-sections.

How each stream was processed

  1. LiDAR: raw scans and GNSS/IMU data → PPK trajectory from the base station → point cloud and strip adjustment → check against ground control and classification → DTM, DSM and contours → sections, slope maps, 3D models and KMZ.
  2. Seismic refraction: SEG-2 shot records → geometry and elevations from LiDAR → first-break picking → delay-time starting model → tomographic inversion → velocity section and interpretation.
  3. Geology: field sheets and photographs → discontinuity database → stereonets and kinematics → RMR, SMR and GSI → Hoek–Brown c′ and φ′ → stability input parameters.

Execution and schedule

The work took about nine weeks from the work order. The field window was about three weeks, with the LiDAR, geology and seismic crews working in parallel in the post-monsoon dry season.

A typical field day ran from a 06:30 toolbox talk (safety, traffic, weather and equipment check), through field work by three crews from 07:00 to 16:30 (the LiDAR crew flew in the calm morning air; the seismic crew shot in gaps in the traffic), to data download, dual backup and quality checks in the evening. The client received a daily progress report: chainage covered, profiles done, issues and the next day's plan.

Safety and environment

  • Roadside work-zone traffic management to IRC:SP:55: cones, signboards, flagmen and reflective jackets at every seismic spread and drone take-off point
  • Drone operations under the Drone Rules, 2021 with Digital Sky permissions; no flying in rain, wind over 10 m/s, poor visibility or over crowds
  • Rock-face mapping only after a check for loose blocks; helmets at all times; an observer watching for rockfall; no climbing without rope access
  • Hammer-source safety: gloves, goggles, ear protection and a clearance zone
  • No tree felling; vegetation cleared by hand only along seismic lines; all waste taken back

Why it worked

  • One team, three disciplines: survey, geophysics and geology planned together and positioned on the same DGPS control, so every data set lines up.
  • LiDAR first, then the ground: a quick-look LiDAR model guided where the seismic lines went, putting effort where the risk was.
  • Built for the design that follows: every deliverable was chosen for the protection design it feeds, not for its own sake.
  • Corridor scale, short field window: parallel crews kept about 66 km of field work to about three weeks.

Frequently asked questions

What did DATA PKT do on the Assam corridor?

We investigated the road-side cut and fill slopes along about 66 km of road in Assam: a drone LiDAR survey of the full corridor, 66 seismic refraction tomography profiles (about one per kilometre), detailed geological mapping at 7 critical locations totalling 2.12 km, and a slope stability analysis with stretch-wise hazard ranking and remedial recommendations.

Why were three methods combined?

Each answers a different question. LiDAR shows the shape of the slope, seismic refraction shows what lies below the surface, and geological mapping shows how the material behaves. Combined in one ground model, they give a factor of safety for each critical section that none of them could give alone.

How long did the investigation take?

About nine weeks from the work order to the final report, with about three weeks in the field. The LiDAR, seismic and geology crews worked in parallel to keep the field window short.

Why is this corridor difficult?

The region gets very heavy monsoon rain, lies in seismic Zone V under IS 1893, and has dense vegetation, soft alluvial soils and weathered sedimentary rock in the cuts. Slopes fail mainly through rain-induced shallow slides, erosion and toe scour, and failures controlled by rock joints.

How were the results used?

The report gave the slope geometry, the thickness of unstable layers, shear strength parameters and failure mechanisms needed to select and dimension flexible high-tensile steel protection systems such as soil-nailed mesh, rockfall drapes and barriers, together with a priority list of where to act first.
