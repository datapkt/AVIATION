Case study · Railways

Yard plans and drainage plans for two railway divisions

For two railway divisions, DATA PKT surveyed station yards with drones and DGPS and turned the data into yard plans and drainage designs: 61 plans submitted in eastern India, and drainage design for 32 yards in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh.

Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026

Eastern India30 yard plans and 31 drainage plans, all submitted
Uttar Pradesh and Madhya PradeshDrainage planning and design for 32 yards
MethodDrone survey with DGPS control, CAD drafting, field checks
Contract periodsSix months each

In short: railways need accurate plans of each yard before remodelling or drainage works. Measuring yards on foot beside running lines is slow and risky. DATA PKT flies each yard, builds an orthomosaic and terrain model, and drafts the yard plan or drainage design from it, with critical details checked on the ground.

Eastern India: 30 yard plans and 31 drainage plans

Under two contracts, each with a six-month completion period, DATA PKT prepared 60+ yard plans (ESPs) and drainage plans in total (30 yard plans and 31 drainage plans), from suburban yards to junctions on the main line, and all of them have been submitted.

Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh: drainage design for 32 yards

A further contract, with a six-month completion period, covers planning and design of drainage in 32 existing yards, from small wayside stations to junctions. The railway asked for LiDAR or an equivalent advanced survey method. DATA PKT has completed the ground survey of all 32 yards, and drainage plans are being prepared and submitted for approval.

For each yard the work covers:

  • A survey report with site conditions and levels
  • An estimate of waste water generated in the yard
  • Drainage slopes and the disposal location
  • Manholes and septic tanks with dispersal trenches, sized for the yard, with a cleaning arrangement
  • Layout plans for the railway's approval

How the work runs

  1. Control: surveyors fix DGPS ground control points around the yard.
  2. Flight: the drone captures the whole yard in one short window, with little time spent beside running lines.
  3. Processing: an orthomosaic and terrain model are built and checked against independent checkpoints.
  4. Drafting: tracks, turnouts, platforms, structures, drains and levels are drawn into the yard plan or drainage design.
  5. Field check and approval: critical details are verified on the ground, and plans are revised until approved.

How a yard survey like this is done

Each of the five steps above is planned around two facts: the yard is beside running lines, and the plan has to show rails, turnouts, drains and small structures in their true positions and at the right levels.

Flight planning

  • GSD from the drawing scale. The ground sample distance (GSD) is set from the plotting scale and the smallest feature to be drawn, and the flight height follows from the camera (GSD = (sensor width × flight height) ÷ (focal length × image width in pixels); see our GSD calculator).
  • Block or corridor. Large junction yards are flown as a block of parallel lines; long, narrow wayside yards are flown as a corridor of lines along the track. Overlap is raised where long rows of wagons, sheds or platform canopies make image matching harder.
  • Short windows beside running lines. Flights are timed with the division, the drone stays well clear of the 25 kV overhead equipment (OHE) and its masts, and take-off points are off the track.
  • Permissions. Flights are planned on the DigitalSky platform under the Drone Rules, 2021, with yellow- or red-zone stretches cleared before the flight date, plus the railway's own written permission for the yard. Pilots hold DGCA Remote Pilot Certificates and drones carry a Unique Identification Number.

Ground control design for a yard

  • Layout. GCPs are placed around the outside of the yard and through its length, off the track and outside the danger zone. For a long, narrow yard they go in pairs on both sides of the station lines, so the model cannot tilt across the yard.
  • Checkpoints. Checkpoints are measured the same way and held out of the adjustment. Some are best placed on rail heads and platform edges, because those are the levels the railway checks.
  • Datum, chainage and levels. Heights are converted from ellipsoidal to orthometric with a geoid model and tied to the railway's benchmark, so that rail levels and drain inverts match the railway's own level datum. Kilometrage is tied to the railway's kilometre posts or chainage reference.

What the air sees, and what the ground must measure

  • Camera: a full-colour, scale-true orthomosaic and terrain model, the base for drafting tracks, turnouts and structures.
  • LiDAR, where specified: laser returns reach the ground through vegetation along overgrown drains and yard edges, where photogrammetry only sees the canopy.
  • Ground instruments: thin, shiny rail heads and the inside of drains and culverts are poorly reconstructed from the air, so rail levels, drain inverts, culvert openings and outfall levels are measured on the ground with a total station, level or GNSS, on the same control.

Processing chain

  1. Aerial triangulation (bundle adjustment) with self-calibration of the camera, and the GCPs fixing the block.
  2. Dense point cloud, DSM and DTM. The digital surface model (DSM) keeps wagons, sheds and trees; the DTM is filtered to ground. LiDAR data, where flown, is classified into ground, vegetation, buildings and noise using the ASPRS LAS 1.4 classes.
  3. Orthomosaic, with the lean of tall structures and canopies checked where it hides track.
  4. Contours and a spot-level grid for the drainage design.

Yard plan (ESP) layers

The yard plan is drafted to the division's layering and title-block standard: running lines, loops and sidings with track centres; turnouts with the position of each switch; platforms, foot over bridges, buildings and goods sheds; bridges, culverts and level crossings; the railway boundary; and chainages. Turnout positions and kilometrage are confirmed on the ground before submission.

Drainage design on the survey

  • Catchments and flow paths from the DTM: where water falls, where it collects (often between tracks and at platform ends), and where it can leave the yard.
  • Storm-water flow by the rational method, Q = C·i·A / 360 (Q in m³/s, C the runoff coefficient, i the rainfall intensity in mm/h for the time of concentration, A in hectares).
  • Drain sizing by Manning's equation, with longitudinal slopes chosen from the surveyed levels so that drains stay self-cleansing at design flow and fall towards the disposal point.
  • Waste water estimated from the yard's users and facilities, commonly with per-capita rates such as those in the CPHEEO Manual on Sewerage and Sewage Treatment Systems (2013).
  • Septic tanks and dispersion trenches sized to IS 2470 (Code of practice for installation of septic tanks), Part 1 for design and construction and Part 2 for secondary treatment and disposal of the effluent, with manholes at changes of direction and gradient and a cleaning arrangement.
  • Drawings: layout plan, longitudinal sections of each drain with invert levels, typical cross-sections, manhole and septic tank details, and the disposal arrangement, for the railway's approval.

How accuracy is reported

Accuracy is reported as root mean square error (RMSE) at independent checkpoints, following the ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023): horizontal accuracy as RMSEH, and vertical accuracy on open, hard ground as non-vegetated vertical accuracy (NVA). Edition 2 reports RMSE alone, without the confidence-level figures of Edition 1, asks for at least 30 checkpoints for a product accuracy assessment, and includes the error of the checkpoint survey itself in the reported figure. A single yard rarely has that many checkpoints, so results are usually given as per-yard checks plus a combined figure across yards.

For drainage, relative accuracy matters most. A drain works if its levels are right relative to each other and to the outfall, so level differences between surveyed points along each drain line are checked, not only absolute accuracy, before slopes are fixed.

Getting the best results

  • Get the division's drawing standard (layers, symbols, title block, chainage convention) before drafting starts; redrawing to a new standard is slow.
  • Ask for the railway's benchmark and kilometrage reference at the start and tie everything to it.
  • Measure on the ground what the air cannot see well: rail levels, drain inverts, culvert openings and outfall levels.
  • Map existing drains and their condition (silted, broken, missing) on the orthomosaic before designing new ones.
  • Check the disposal point first: a design that cannot discharge by gravity changes everything upstream.
  • Keep the orthomosaic and terrain model with the drawings; they answer later queries without another site visit.
  • For drainage design, survey a little beyond the railway boundary to see where water enters and leaves the yard.

Why drones are faster

A ground survey with DGPS and total station measures one point at a time, so a long corridor takes weeks. A DATA PKT drone team captures around 5 sq km of survey data in a day, which is then mapped and analysed in the office.

On a linear corridor such as a highway, one team captures about 35 km of drone imagery for photogrammetry, or about 50 km of aerial video, in a day, and DATA PKT has more than 20 drone pilots (a team of about 22) to put in the field. Ground instruments are still used, but only where they matter most: for the control points that fix the map to real coordinates, for checkpoints that prove the accuracy, and for detail the drone cannot see.

Frequently asked questions

How many railway yard plans has DATA PKT prepared?

For one railway division in eastern India we submitted 30 yard plans and 31 drainage plans. For a second division, in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh, we have surveyed all 32 yards in the contract and are preparing their drainage designs.

What does a railway yard drainage plan include?

Ground levels and slopes, the estimated waste water from the yard, the disposal location, and the design of drains, manholes and septic tanks with dispersal trenches, with a cleaning arrangement.

Why survey railway yards with drones?

A drone captures a whole yard in one short window, so staff spend far less time on or beside running lines, and the orthomosaic and terrain model stay on file for later questions.

Can rail levels be measured from a drone survey?

Rail heads are narrow and reflective, so photogrammetry reconstructs them poorly. A drone survey gives the yard layout and the ground surface; rail levels and other critical levels, such as drain inverts and outfalls, should be measured on the ground and tied to the same control.

Which height datum should a yard drainage design use?

The same one as the railway's existing drawings and benchmarks. GNSS gives heights above the ellipsoid, which must be converted to orthometric heights with a geoid model and checked on a known benchmark before any drain slope is designed.
