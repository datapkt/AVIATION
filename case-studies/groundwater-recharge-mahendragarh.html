Case study · Water and irrigation

Groundwater recharge system for Mahendragarh: survey and design of two canal-fed lines

In Mahendragarh district, in southern Haryana, DATA PKT is surveying and designing a groundwater recharge system. Two lines, of about 45 km and 15 km, take water from an existing canal or river and carry it to structures that return it to the aquifer.

LocationMahendragarh (Mahindergarh) district, Haryana
PurposeRecharge groundwater with surplus canal or river water
NetworkTwo lines, about 45 km and 15 km long
Our scopeDrone and DGPS survey, planning and design of the recharge system

In short: water that a canal or river carries in surplus is drawn off, carried along two lines and put back into the ground through recharge structures. DATA PKT surveys the corridors with drones and DGPS and designs the system on that survey: the off-take, the alignment and levels of each line, the crossings and the recharge structures.

The need

Mahendragarh is a semi-arid district in southern Haryana. Farms and villages draw most of their water from the ground, rainfall is short and seasonal, and groundwater levels have been falling for years. Canals and rivers carry more water at some times of the year than is used. A recharge system stores that surplus underground, where it does not evaporate and can be pumped back by wells across the area.

How the recharge system works

The system is designed as a chain, from the source to the aquifer. Each link is sized from the survey levels and the water that can be drawn:

  1. Off-take at the canal or river: an intake with a regulator or gate, so water is drawn only when it is available and in the quantity allowed.
  2. Silt control: a desilting or sedimentation chamber and filter, because silt that reaches a recharge well clogs it.
  3. Conveyance lines: the two lines, about 45 km and 15 km, laid to an alignment and gradient that move water by gravity wherever the ground allows.
  4. Crossings: structures where the lines cross roads, railways, other canals, drains and utilities.
  5. Recharge structures: recharge wells and shafts, percolation pits and recharge ponds, and existing village ponds revived to hold and soak water, placed where the ground can take it.
  6. Escapes and monitoring: outlets for excess water, and points to watch water levels so the effect can be measured.

How drones make the design possible

A recharge network is a long, linear project across farmland and villages. Designing it needs the levels and features of every kilometre, and a conventional ground survey of 60 km would take weeks. A drone survey tied to DGPS ground control captures the corridors in days and gives the designers the whole picture at once.

How a recharge-line survey like this is done

A gravity line across nearly flat farmland can have a fall of only a few metres over many kilometres, so a survey for a recharge network is planned around vertical accuracy, not picture detail.

Flight planning

  • GSD from the design need. The ground sample distance is chosen for the vertical accuracy the levels need, and the flight height follows from the camera (GSD = (sensor width × flight height) ÷ (focal length × image width in pixels); see our GSD calculator).
  • Corridors, not a block. The 45 km and 15 km lines are flown as corridors wide enough to shift the alignment around houses, wells and trees without another flight.
  • Wider blocks at key sites. The off-take, major crossings and candidate recharge sites (low ground and village ponds) are flown as small blocks around the corridor, so the structures can be laid out on them.
  • Season and crops. Standing crops hide the ground from photogrammetry. Flights are best timed after harvest, and areas still under crop or trees are covered by ground levels or LiDAR.
  • Airspace. Flights are planned on the DigitalSky platform under the Drone Rules, 2021, and flown by DGCA Remote Pilot Certificate holders on drones with a Unique Identification Number. Stretches in yellow or red zones are cleared before the flight date.

Ground control and levels

  • Control along both corridors. Static GNSS control pillars form the backbone, with GCP pairs on alternate sides of each corridor.
  • Checkpoints are held out of the adjustment and placed on open, hard ground (roads, canal bunds, pads), so that they test vertical accuracy fairly.
  • Benchmarks for levels. Temporary benchmarks along the lines are tied by levelling to a Survey of India or irrigation department benchmark, so the levels at the off-take, at each crossing and at each recharge structure are in one system.
  • Orthometric heights. GNSS ellipsoidal heights are converted with a geoid model and checked on the benchmarks, so canal bed and water levels from the irrigation department's records can be compared directly with the survey.

Processing chain

  1. Aerial triangulation (bundle adjustment), with GCPs fixing each corridor block.
  2. Dense point cloud and DSM; ground filtering to a DTM, edited by hand where crops, hedges or houses remain. LiDAR, if flown, is classified to the ASPRS LAS 1.4 classes (ground, vegetation, buildings, water, noise).
  3. Orthomosaic and contours.
  4. Alignment trials, each with a longitudinal section and cross-sections at regular intervals.
  5. Khasra (revenue) maps georeferenced to the orthomosaic, for the land each kilometre crosses.

Hydraulic design on the survey

  • Water available at the source. The design flow is set from the surplus the canal or river can release and for how long, usually from the irrigation department's discharge records.
  • Head available. The surveyed level at the off-take minus the level at each recharge site gives the head that can move water by gravity.
  • Line sizing. Open channels are sized by Manning's equation; pipelines by Darcy–Weisbach or Hazen–Williams head-loss calculations. Where the surveyed head is not enough, the longitudinal section shows where pumping or a change of route is needed.
  • Crossings are drawn on the cross-sections at each road, railway, canal, drain and service line, with the levels and clearances the crossing owner needs.

Choosing recharge sites

Recharge structures only work where water can move from the surface into the aquifer. Sites are usually chosen in two passes:

  1. GIS screening. Layers for slope (from the DTM), low ground and ponds (from the DTM and orthomosaic), land use, soil, geology and geomorphology, drainage, and depth to water level are combined in a weighted overlay to rank candidate sites. This follows the approach of the Central Ground Water Board's Manual on Artificial Recharge of Ground Water (2007) and its Master Plan for Artificial Recharge to Groundwater in India (2020).
  2. Field confirmation. Shortlisted sites are confirmed on the ground, typically with double-ring infiltration tests (ASTM D3385), electrical resistivity soundings for the thickness of the unsaturated zone and the aquifer, and a check of existing wells.
  • Structure type follows the ground: recharge shafts or wells where a clay layer near the surface would stop water soaking in; percolation pits and recharge ponds where permeable soil is near the surface; existing village ponds revived where they already collect water.
  • Silt and water quality: desilting chambers and filters are sized for the expected silt load, because recharge must not carry silt or contaminants into the aquifer.

How accuracy is reported

Accuracy is reported as root mean square error (RMSE) at independent checkpoints, following the ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023): horizontal accuracy as RMSEH, and vertical accuracy on open, hard ground as non-vegetated vertical accuracy (NVA). Edition 2 reports RMSE alone, without the confidence-level figures of Edition 1, asks for at least 30 checkpoints for a product accuracy assessment, and includes the error of the checkpoint survey itself in the reported figure. For cropped and scrub areas, vegetated vertical accuracy (VVA) is reported as well; under Edition 2 it is reported without a pass or fail threshold.

What decides gravity flow is relative accuracy: whether the level at one end of a line is right relative to the level at the other. Levelling along the benchmarks, closed within tolerance, checks the drone terrain model along the full length.

Getting the best results

  • Survey the off-take first; its levels decide whether the rest of the line can work by gravity.
  • Fly after harvest where the season allows; it is cheaper than editing crops out of the terrain model.
  • Tie the survey to the irrigation department's benchmark so its canal records can be used directly.
  • Keep the corridor wide enough to move the alignment around houses, wells and trees without re-flying.
  • Map existing ponds, wells and drains on the orthomosaic before choosing new recharge sites.
  • Treat desilting as part of the design, not an add-on: a recharge well clogged with silt stops working.
  • Plan monitoring points with the design, so the effect on groundwater levels can be measured afterwards.

Why drones are faster

A ground survey with DGPS and total station measures one point at a time, so a long corridor takes weeks. A DATA PKT drone team captures around 5 sq km of survey data in a day, which is then mapped and analysed in the office.

On a linear corridor such as a highway, one team captures about 35 km of drone imagery for photogrammetry, or about 50 km of aerial video, in a day, and DATA PKT has more than 20 drone pilots (a team of about 22) to put in the field. Ground instruments are still used, but only where they matter most: for the control points that fix the map to real coordinates, for checkpoints that prove the accuracy, and for detail the drone cannot see.

How we run the survey and design

  1. Desk study: canal and river records, existing maps and groundwater information for the area.
  2. Reconnaissance: the off-take point, the possible routes and the recharge sites are walked and photographed.
  3. DGPS control: ground control points and benchmarks are set along both corridors, so every map and level is tied to real coordinates.
  4. Drone mapping: the 45 km and 15 km corridors are flown and processed into orthomosaics, terrain models and contours.
  5. Ground survey: levels at the canal or river, at crossings and at structure sites are checked with DGPS and total station.
  6. Design: alignment, longitudinal sections, hydraulic sizing of the lines, and drawings of the off-take, silt chambers, crossings and recharge structures.
  7. Hand-over: drawings, sections, quantities and the survey data, in CAD and GIS formats.

Frequently asked questions

What is a groundwater recharge system?

A network that takes surplus water from a canal or river when it is available and lets it soak back into the aquifer through recharge structures such as recharge wells and shafts, percolation pits and ponds, instead of letting it run off or evaporate.

What is DATA PKT doing in Mahendragarh?

We are carrying out the survey and design of a groundwater recharge system for the Mahendragarh area in Haryana. Two lines, of about 45 km and 15 km, take water from an existing canal or river and carry it to the recharge structures.

How do drones help design a recharge system?

A drone survey tied to DGPS control maps the whole corridor as an orthomosaic and terrain model in days. Designers use it to choose the alignment, check that water can flow by gravity, draw longitudinal and cross-sections, find crossings and pick recharge sites such as low ground and village ponds.

How long does it take to survey a 45 km line?

One DATA PKT drone team captures about 35 km of linear corridor imagery in a day, so the flying for both lines takes a few days. DGPS control, ground checks and processing come on top.

Does DATA PKT survey canals and rivers for irrigation departments?

Yes. We survey canals, distributaries, minors, drains and rivers: longitudinal and cross-sections, bathymetry of the water channel, structures such as regulators, falls and bridges, encroachments, lining condition and command areas.

Why does a recharge line survey need such accurate levels?

An open gravity channel flows only if its bed falls continuously; a gravity pipeline flows only if the head at the off-take is more than the level at the outlet plus the friction losses along the way. Either way the design rests on level differences. On flat farmland the total fall over many kilometres can be small, so a vertical error that would not matter on a map can decide whether the line flows, needs pumping or has to be rerouted.

Can a drone survey see the ground under crops?

Photogrammetry maps the top of whatever covers the ground, so standing crops and trees raise the surface. Surveys are best timed after harvest, and remaining areas are covered by ground levels or LiDAR, whose laser returns can reach the ground through gaps in vegetation.
