220/132 kV substation and transmission lines in Uttar Pradesh

For a railway traction power project in Uttar Pradesh, DATA PKT is carrying out the survey and design of a new 220/132 kV grid substation, the 220 kV and 132 kV lines that feed it, and location plans for 19 sites along the railway.

Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026

LocationUttar Pradesh
Substation220/132 kV grid substation (GSS)
Lines220 kV D/C (32 m ROW) and 132 kV D/C (25 m ROW)
StatusIn progress; six-month programme

In short: the project needs power for 2x25 kV railway traction. DATA PKT finds three alternate routes for each new line, compares them on maps made from current drone data laid over khasra maps, surveys the approved route in detail, investigates and designs the substation, and prepares location plans for traction substations and sectioning posts.

Scope

PartWhat DATA PKT delivers

132 kV D/C line, new GSS to an existing 132 kV lineThree alternate routes with the LILO (loop-in loop-out) point on the existing line, then detailed survey
220/132 kV GSSContour survey, geotechnical investigation, soil resistivity, HFL and finished ground level

Three alternate routes, compared on current drone maps

For each line the client asked for three alternate routes and a comparative feasibility report with a techno-economic recommendation. The usual starting point is Survey of India toposheets, satellite imagery and revenue maps, but these are often years out of date. DATA PKT flies each corridor with drone photogrammetry, so every route is compared on how the ground looks today.

  • Route length, angle points and deviation angles
  • Crossings: rivers and canals, national and state highways, railways, power lines from LT to 765 kV, telecom and optical fibre
  • Forest, settlements and obligatory points
  • Right-of-way sensitivity for each alternative

Drone maps on khasra maps: land acquisition made clear

The drone orthomosaic of each corridor is superimposed on the khasra (revenue) maps. The khasra gives the plot numbers; the drone data shows what is on each plot today. Together they show, for every alternative:

  • Which plots the line and each tower would fall on
  • Whether the land is residential, commercial, agricultural or forest
  • Where a small shift in an angle point avoids houses, shops or sensitive land
  • The land acquisition and right-of-way picture before the route is approved

That turns route approval from a debate over old maps into a comparison of facts, and makes the land acquisition (LA) work that follows far easier.

How a line survey like this is done

Two survey stages, two accuracy levels

  1. Route alternatives (reconnaissance mapping). Each alternate corridor is flown wide enough either side of the trial alignment for angle points to move without a second flight. The aim is a current, georeferenced picture for the route comparison and land planning.
  2. Approved route (detailed survey). The approved route is surveyed in detail with DGPS, total station and levelling for the profile, tower spotting and schedules, with the drone data as the base for plan features and crossings.

Flight planning

  • Height from GSD. Flight height is set from the target ground sample distance and the camera (GSD = (sensor width × flight height) ÷ (focal length × image width in pixels); see our GSD calculator).
  • Corridor lines. Parallel flight lines run along each corridor, and the corridor is broken into segments at each angle point so that the lines stay parallel to the alignment.
  • Terrain following where the ground height changes along the route, so the GSD stays near the target.
  • Existing lines. Corridors cross existing power lines from LT to EHV. The drone keeps well clear of energised conductors, and crossing spans are flown higher or measured from the ground.
  • Airspace. Flights are planned on the DigitalSky platform under the Drone Rules, 2021, and flown by DGCA Remote Pilot Certificate holders on drones with a Unique Identification Number. Stretches in yellow or red zones are cleared before the flight date.

Ground control design for a line corridor

  • GCP layout. GCP pairs on alternate sides of the corridor, with extra points at angle points, at the substation ends and at major crossings, where the profile matters most.
  • Checkpoints measured like GCPs and held out of the adjustment.
  • Control network. Static GNSS control pillars form the backbone, and the angle points and reference points are coordinated (X, Y, Z) from this network.
  • One height system. Heights are orthometric, converted from ellipsoidal heights with a geoid model and tied to a benchmark, and the HFL and rail levels used for the substation's finished ground level are in the same height system.

Processing chain

  1. Aerial triangulation (bundle adjustment), with GCPs fixing each corridor block.
  2. Dense matching, DSM, ground-filtered DTM and orthomosaic.
  3. Where LiDAR is flown: trajectory processing, strip adjustment and classification to the ASPRS LAS 1.4 classes, including ground (class 2), vegetation (3 to 5), buildings (6) and, for existing lines, wire guard or shield wire (13), wire conductor (14) and transmission tower (15).
  4. Khasra maps georeferenced to the orthomosaic using boundary points that can be identified on both, with the residuals recorded. Where a revenue map does not fit the ground, the misfit is reported rather than the orthomosaic being stretched to match it.

Line design on the profile

  • Longitudinal profile of the approved route, with ground levels, crossings and obstacles along the centre line, and at the outer conductor offsets where side slopes are steep.
  • Tower spotting with a sag template. Towers are placed on the profile with a sag template cut for the ruling span and conductor: the hot curve (maximum sag at maximum conductor temperature, still air); the ground clearance curve (the hot curve dropped by the minimum ground clearance); the tower footing curve (the hot curve dropped by the conductor attachment height of the standard tower); and the cold curve (minimum temperature, to check uplift on towers in low ground).
  • Clearances. Ground clearance at maximum sag is checked against the minimum clearances in the Central Electricity Authority (Measures relating to Safety and Electric Supply) Regulations, 2023. Crossings over railways, highways and other power lines are checked against the requirements of the owner of each crossing, and against any higher values in the project's design basis.
  • Schedules and plans: the tower schedule, the crossing schedule, the route alignment plan on the orthomosaic, and tree and crop enumeration within the right of way.

Quality checks

  • Drone maps: Accuracy is reported as root mean square error (RMSE) at independent checkpoints, following the ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, Edition 2 (2023): horizontal accuracy as RMSEH, and vertical accuracy on open, hard ground as non-vegetated vertical accuracy (NVA). Edition 2 reports RMSE alone, without the confidence-level figures of Edition 1, asks for at least 30 checkpoints for a product accuracy assessment, and includes the error of the checkpoint survey itself in the reported figure.
  • Control network: loop closures and a network adjustment report.
  • Profile levels: levelling loops closed within the specified tolerance.
  • Absolute and relative accuracy. Tower coordinates need absolute accuracy so towers can be set out on the ground. The profile needs relative vertical accuracy along each span, because clearance is the difference between conductor and ground levels.

Getting the best results

  • Fly the alternatives early: a current map of each corridor settles many route questions before detailed survey starts.
  • Keep the reconnaissance corridor wide enough for angle points to move without a second flight.
  • Get the revenue maps for every village on the corridors before the comparison, and check their fit against the orthomosaic.
  • Measure existing line heights at every crossing and record the conductor temperature or time of day.
  • Take side-slope levels wherever the ground falls across the line, not only along the centre line.
  • Confirm the design basis (conductor, ruling span, maximum temperature, clearances) with the client before tower spotting.
  • Put HFL, rail levels and survey levels in one height system before fixing the substation's finished ground level.

Detailed survey of the approved route

  • DGPS and total station survey with levelling, and coordinates (X, Y, Z) of every angle and reference point
  • Longitudinal profiles (horizontal 1 cm = 20 m, vertical 1 cm = 2 m) with tower spotting and optimisation to IS 5613 and IS 802
  • Tower schedules (type, extension, span, deviation angle, chainage, ground level) and the route alignment plan
  • Crossing schedules with chainage, crossing angle and clearance
  • Tree and crop enumeration along the corridor

Substation investigations and design

At the GSS site DATA PKT carries out a contour survey, boreholes with standard penetration tests and laboratory testing for the geotechnical report, and Wenner four-electrode soil resistivity tests to IS 3043 for earthing design. HFL data and rail levels set the recommended finished ground level.

The design work covers the general arrangement drawing (GAD) of the 220/132 kV switchyard (bus-bars, transformers, breakers, isolators, CTs, CVTs, lightning arresters, gantries, roads, drains and fire fighting), the GAD of the GSS building, the general power-supply diagram of the 220 kV, 132 kV and 2x25 kV network, and the LT wiring plan, LT single line diagram and distribution board schedules. The final GAD is vetted by an IIT, NIT or CPRI.

Location plans for 19 sites

Along the railway, DATA PKT surveys the GSS plot and each proposed traction substation, sectioning post and sub-sectioning post and prepares its location plan with boundary, area, levels, utilities and railway chainage. The 19 sites are the new GSS, 4 traction substations and 14 sectioning and sub-sectioning posts across two sections.

Why drones are faster

A ground survey with DGPS and total station measures one point at a time, so a long corridor takes weeks. A DATA PKT drone team captures around 5 sq km of survey data in a day, which is then mapped and analysed in the office.

On a linear corridor such as a highway, one team captures about 35 km of drone imagery for photogrammetry, or about 50 km of aerial video, in a day, and DATA PKT has more than 20 drone pilots (a team of about 22) to put in the field. Ground instruments are still used, but only where they matter most: for the control points that fix the map to real coordinates, for checkpoints that prove the accuracy, and for detail the drone cannot see.

Frequently asked questions

What does this project cover?

Survey and design of a new 220/132 kV grid substation (GSS) in Uttar Pradesh, a 220 kV double-circuit line from the state grid to the GSS, a 132 kV double-circuit line from the GSS to an existing line, and location plans for 19 sites: the substation, 4 traction substations and 14 sectioning and sub-sectioning posts.

How do drones help choose a transmission line route?

Drone photogrammetry maps each alternate corridor as it is today. Laid over khasra (revenue) maps, it shows which plots each route crosses and what is on them: farmland, houses, shops or forest. That makes the route comparison and land acquisition planning far easier.

Does DATA PKT design substations?

Yes. The scope includes the general arrangement drawing (GAD) of the 220/132 kV switchyard, the GAD of the substation building, the power-supply diagram, the LT single line diagram and LT wiring plan, vetted by an IIT, NIT or CPRI.

Is the project complete?

No. It is in progress, with field survey under way in October 2026 and a six-month programme from award.

What is a sag template and why is it used?

A sag template is a curve of the conductor's shape at a chosen temperature, cut to the profile's scales. Laid on the ground profile, it shows where towers can stand so that the conductor keeps the required clearance at maximum sag, and, with the cold curve, whether any tower in low ground would be pulled upward.

Can a drone survey replace the detailed survey of a transmission line?

Not entirely. Drone mapping gives a current base for comparing routes, land planning, crossings and plan features. The tower schedule and profile still rely on control, levelling and total station work on the approved route, tied to the same coordinates.
